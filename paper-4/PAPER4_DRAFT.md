# Three Numbers per Rater: Predictable Individual Differences in Preference Data Are Largely Scale Use

## Abstract (draft)
Pluralistic alignment trains reward models that represent disagreement among people — as a distribution over ratings, as demographic groups, or as a learned user latent. All three assume that what makes people disagree is something a model should learn from the text or from who the people are. We measure, on three public multi-rater datasets (MultiPref pairwise preferences, DICES conversational-safety ratings, PRISM response ratings), where the reproducible structure of disagreement actually sits. Split-half analysis shows that the shape of the rating distribution beyond its location is poorly reproducible across rater panels (split-half reliability 0.25 on MultiPref), that about a tenth of the reproducible part is decodable from a frozen LLM representation of the text, and that, beyond item location, rater-side scale use improves held-out likelihood about 25 times more than item-specific dispersion (19–36 across folds). Acting on this, we model each rater by a few text-independent offsets on the reward model's output — how they use the scale — learned from a handful of their ratings. For raters never seen in training, offsets fitted on 20 ratings improve held-out log-likelihood over a distributional reward model by 0.08–0.10 nats per rating on all three datasets, while demographic or crowd/expert groups improve it by at most 0.003, a tuned 8-dimensional text-interacting user latent matches but does not exceed them, and reimplementations of two published personalised reward models (VPL, PAL) gain less; on binary preference labels every method's personalisation gain is at most 0.002 nats. On pairwise preferences, a symmetric three-parameter version (how often a rater says "clearly", "slightly", "tie") is best or tied-best at every k ≥ 5 and slightly improves predicting which response the rater prefers; adding an A-vs-B position term from few ratings hurts. In an interpretable probit analogue, rater scale dominates on two-sided preference scales and rater shift (leniency) on single-response ratings. On top of a LoRA-fine-tuned reward model the gain is unchanged (+0.100) and adds to the fine-tuning gain, and it exceeds an in-context-personalised LoRA reward model on the same base model (+0.042; two folds, one seed).

## 1. Introduction
Reward models and LLM judges are trained on human ratings that disagree. A recent line of work treats this disagreement as signal to be modelled rather than noise to be averaged: distributional preference learning estimates a distribution over each alternative's utility rather than a point score (Siththaranjan et al., 2024); jury learning predicts individual annotators from content, annotator and demographic-group embeddings (Gordon et al., 2022), and large datasets such as PRISM are analysed by sociodemographic group (Kirk et al., 2024); personalised reward models learn a per-user latent, inferred for a new user from a few examples (Poddar et al., 2024; Chen et al., 2025). The pluralistic-alignment agenda (Sorensen et al., 2024) makes the representation of disagreement a first-class goal.

These approaches agree on one premise: the disagreement worth modelling is a property of the *item* (some responses are contested) or of *who the raters are* (groups and individuals hold different values about content). There is a third, older possibility that measurement theory takes for granted and reward modelling largely does not: raters differ in **how they use the response scale** — some say "clearly better" where others say "slightly better", some rarely call a tie, some are harsher across the board (Linacre, 1989; Baumgartner & Steenkamp, 2001). Such response styles are text-independent, cheap to estimate, and invisible to a model that only conditions on content or demographics.

We ask two questions. **(Q1, measurement)** In public multi-rater preference and rating data, how much of the disagreement is reproducible across raters, how much of the reproducible part is in the text, and how much is rater-side? **(Q2, modelling)** If the answer is "mostly rater-side", what is the cheapest rater model that captures it for a *new* rater from few ratings — and how does it compare with distributional, group-conditioned and low-rank personalised reward models under the same protocol?

**Contributions.** (1) A learner-free measurement of disagreement in preference data — split-half reliability of the distribution's shape, cross-fitted text decodability, and a rater–item decomposition of held-out likelihood — showing that, beyond item location, rater-side scale use carries most of the predictable structure (§3). (2) A factorised reward model: a text model for the item, and a few text-independent offsets per rater on its output, shrunk toward the population and adapted from k ratings (§4). (3) A new-rater, few-shot evaluation on three datasets against distributional, group-conditioned and low-rank-latent reward models, with hypotheses specified in timestamped internal protocols, post-result additions marked as amendments, and failures reported (§5–6): offsets beat groups everywhere, match a tuned low-rank latent, and a symmetric three-number style model is best or tied-best on pairwise preferences. (4) In an interpretable probit analogue, the dominant style component depends on the scale: intensity on two-sided preferences, leniency on single-response ratings (§5.3).

## 2. Related work
**Pluralistic and distributional reward models.** Distributional preference learning (Siththaranjan et al., 2024), diverging-preference detection (Zhang et al., 2025), PRISM (Kirk et al., 2024), MultiPref (Miranda et al., 2025) and DICES (Aroyo et al., 2023) motivate modelling disagreement; jury learning (Gordon et al., 2022) and multi-annotator models (Davani et al., 2022) predict individual annotators. **Personalised reward models** infer a user latent from a few examples (Poddar et al., 2024; Chen et al., 2025) or learn per-user embeddings jointly with the reward model (Li et al., 2024). **Annotator demographics** as predictors of labels have been found weak in several studies (e.g. Orlikowski et al., 2023). **Measurement theory**: rater severity and scale use are standard facets in many-facet Rasch measurement (Linacre, 1989; Eckes, 2011) and response styles are a classical survey-methods topic (Baumgartner & Steenkamp, 2001). We connect the last line to the first: for new-rater prediction, the measurement-theory rater facet is what the reward model is missing.

## 3. Where does disagreement in preference data sit? (Q1)
**Data.** MultiPref (Miranda et al., 2025; 10,461 comparisons; 4 ratings each by 2 crowd and 2 expert raters; 227 persistent raters; 5 ordered levels A-clearly … B-clearly). PRISM (Kirk et al., 2024; 68,371 responses each rated 1–100 by one of 1,396 users; binned to 5 fixed levels). DICES-350 (Aroyo et al., 2023; 350 conversations fully crossed with 104 quality-checked raters; safe / unsure / unsafe).

**Estimators.** For an item with ratings on $K$ ordered levels, let $h$ be the empirical rating distribution and $C$ the cumulative operator. Split the item's raters at random into disjoint panels $P_1$ ($a$ raters) and $P_2$, and let $q$ be a cross-fitted *location-only* forecast with exactly the mode and mean of $P_1$ (built from other items in the same mode-and-mean cell). With $r_1 = C(h_1 - q)$, $r_2 = C(h_2 - q)$, $M = \|r_1\|^2/n$ and $X = \langle r_1, r_2\rangle/n$, the forecasting value of the raw distribution over its own location obeys the identity $S(h_1) - S(q) = M - 2X$ under the ranked probability score, so $\rho = X/M$ is a split-half reliability of the distribution's *shape* and the raw distribution beats its location exactly when $\rho > 1/2$. Text decodability is the proper-score gain $G = (2\langle \hat r, r_2\rangle - \|\hat r\|^2)/n$ of a residual $\hat r$ decoded from frozen text features, cross-fitted over prompt groups, reported as the fraction $G/X$ of the reproducible shape. The rater–item decomposition fits ordered-probit models $Y^*_{ir} = \theta_i + \delta_r + \sigma_i\sigma_r\varepsilon$ with item locations $\theta_i$, rater shifts $\delta_r$, rater scales $\sigma_r$ and item dispersions $\sigma_i$, compares held-out likelihood over random cells (every held-out rater and item appears in training), and chooses all penalties by inner cross-validation.

**Reproducibility.** With the split-half estimator of [companion paper, anonymised], the reliability of the rating distribution's shape beyond its exact mode and mean is ρ = 0.25 [0.22, 0.29] on MultiPref overall preference with three raters (helpful 0.29, harmless 0.21): the raw few-rater distribution forecasts a new rater worse than its own location (ρ < ½).
**Text decodability.** A cross-fitted ridge decoder on frozen Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct features, scored on an independent rater panel, recovers G/X = 10.5 % [5.7 %, 11.9 %] of the reproducible shape (within-cell permutation control ≤ 0; positive control 0.35) — comparable to the upper end for ordinal quality ratings (upper bounds 0.4–10 %) and below NLI (17–26 %).
**Rater vs item.** A long-format ordered-probit decomposition with real rater identities and penalties tuned by inner cross-validation: rater-specific thresholds improve held-out likelihood by +0.036 nats per rating over item location alone, item-specific dispersion by a further +0.0014 (ratio 25; 19–36 across the five folds); of the rater part, rater scale contributes +0.031 and rater shift +0.005.

Beyond item location, the predictable structure is therefore mostly rater-side scale use, while the item-level shape that replicates across disjoint panels is small and only partly in the text. These two facts come from different estimands and do not say that disagreement is "not in the text". The stage-1 decision rule we had written down returned **"mixed"**, because G/X was not ≤ 10 % (interval 5.7–11.9 %); the factorised model of §4 was chosen with that outcome known.

## 4. A factorised reward model (Q2)
Let ℓ(x) ∈ R^K be the class logits of a text-only distributional reward model for item x (K ordered rating levels). For rater r we add a text-independent offset vector: p(y | x, r) = softmax(ℓ(x) + b_r). Variants: **DF** (b_r ∈ R^K, free), **DFs** (two-sided preference scales only: b_r tied symmetrically, (e, s, t, s, e) — extreme, slight, tie — 3 parameters, no A-vs-B position term). Rater offsets have a Gaussian prior centred on the training-rater mean; a new rater's offsets are the MAP estimate from k of their ratings with the text model frozen. Baselines under the same protocol: **D** (text-only distributional RM), **DG** (group offsets: crowd/expert, race, region), **DL** (low-rank text-interacting latent: ℓ(x) + (Wx + w₀)·z_r, d = 8, the personalised-RM family). An interpretable probit family (rater shift / log-scale on an ordered-probit text head) is reported as a secondary analysis.

**Training and adaptation.** The text model and the training raters' offsets are fitted jointly by penalised maximum likelihood,
$$\min \sum_{(i,r,y)} -\log p(y \mid x_i, r) + \lambda_{\text{text}} \|V\|^2 + \lambda_r \sum_r \|b_r\|^2,$$
with $\lambda_{\text{text}}$ chosen on the text-only model and $\lambda_r$ on held-out items of the training raters. A new rater $r^*$ is adapted from $k$ ratings $\{(x_j, y_j)\}$ by $\hat b_{r^*} = \arg\min_b \sum_j -\log \mathrm{softmax}(\ell(x_j) + b)_{y_j} + \lambda_r \|b - \bar b\|^2$, with $\bar b$ the training-rater mean (the prior mean; $\hat b = \bar b$ at $k = 0$) and the text model frozen — a convex problem in 3–5 parameters, solved in milliseconds. Any text model that outputs graded log-probabilities can be used: we use a ridge head on frozen LLM features (§5.1–5.3), a LoRA-fine-tuned LLM reward model (§5.4) and the decoder of a published personalised reward model (§5.5).

## 5. Experiments
**Protocol.** 5 folds; in each, 20 % of raters (stratified by group) and 20 % of prompt groups are held out; models are trained on training raters × training items with penalties chosen on a validation split; each test rater is adapted on k ∈ {0, 5, 10, 20, 50} of their ratings of *training* items (5 random draws) and evaluated on their ratings of *held-out* items. Primary metric: held-out log-likelihood per rating; also RPS and preference-direction accuracy; rater-cluster bootstrap (B = 2,000). Text model in §5.1–5.3: frozen Qwen2.5-7B features with a ridge head; §5.4: a LoRA-fine-tuned reward model.

### 5.3 What the offsets encode
In the probit family, rater *scale* matters more than rater *shift* on MultiPref (+0.036 [+0.020, +0.057] at k = 20), but rater *shift* dominates on DICES (−0.053) and PRISM (−0.031): leniency/strictness on single-response rating scales, intensity on two-sided preference scales.

### 5.4 On a fine-tuned reward model
With a LoRA-fine-tuned Qwen2.5-7B reward model (D-LoRA, trained per fold on the training raters' rating distributions) as the text model, the three-number style model still adds +0.100 [+0.071, +0.130] nats per rating at k = 20 — the same as its gain on the frozen-feature head (+0.100). Fine-tuning improves the text-only model by +0.027 [+0.016, +0.039] (direction accuracy +1.4 points) and the style model by the same +0.027: the two gains add. Personalising the frozen-feature model with 20 ratings per rater gains more than fine-tuning the text model (+0.072 [+0.041, +0.106]). DFs keeps the direction accuracy of D-LoRA (+0.002 [−0.002, +0.006]). Against in-context personalisation — a separate LoRA model on the same base, trained with ten of the rater's other ratings in the prompt and evaluated with the new rater's k ratings in the prompt (two folds, one training seed per fold, 86–90 raters; the in-context gain varies across folds, +0.075 and +0.021) — the three offsets on D-LoRA are better at k = 5 (+0.041 [+0.016, +0.065] nats per rating) and at k = 20 (+0.042 [+0.020, +0.065]), and more accurate on which response the rater prefers (+2.9 points); the in-context model learns from the history (+0.048 over D-LoRA) but loses 3.0 points of direction accuracy, at several thousand prompt tokens per prediction.

### 5.5 Published personalised reward models
We reimplemented VPL (Poddar et al., 2024) and PAL-B (Chen et al., 2025) from their official code as heads on the same frozen per-response embeddings and evaluated them under the same new-rater protocol, refitting the text-only and style models on those embeddings. On graded labels, at k = 20 the style model is better than VPL by +0.092 [+0.068, +0.119] nats per rating and than PAL-B by +0.158. VPL's latent does carry rater information once its posterior collapse is prevented (KL weight selected as 0 or 0.001; +0.087 over its own unpersonalised version), but only lifts it to the level of the text-only model; PAL-B's few-shot user weights give no gain. Their user representations are unrelated to response style (cross-validated R² ≈ 0 against the style offsets). On binary labels — the setting in which both methods were proposed — every method's personalisation gain is at most 0.002 nats (small but nonzero for PAL-B with K = 4 and for VPL at k = 50), and VPL's text head is the best binary model (the style model is 0.010 nats behind it and 0.005 behind the text-only model): the individual differences that are predictable for new raters live in the graded levels and ties that binary preference data discard. VPL's per-response reward head is slightly better at predicting *which* response is preferred (0.677 vs 0.668), an effect present without personalisation. The offsets are also a plug-in: added to VPL (fitted on the same k ratings, VPL frozen) they improve it by +0.088 [+0.066, +0.111] nats per rating at k = 20, tie with the style model on likelihood (−0.004 [−0.014, +0.005]) and keep VPL's direction accuracy (+1.1 points over the style model alone).

## 6. Protocols and what did not hold
All analyses followed timestamped internal protocols (not public registrations); changes after results are logged as amendments with reasons, and the symmetric style model DFs and its hypothesis were added after the first full result. Not supported or not robust: (i) the stage-1 decision rule returned "mixed" (text decodability not ≤ 10 %); (ii) non-inferiority of the 5-parameter DF to the low-rank latent (H3: DF − DL ≥ −0.005) holds with the training-rater prior but not with a few-shot-tuned prior on MultiPref (−0.015) and is formally not met on PRISM (bound −0.006); (iii) "scale beats shift" (H4) holds only on MultiPref; (iv) the originally registered probit-family H5 is not supported (F − D = −0.029 [−0.049, −0.008]; superseded by the distributional family in Amendment 2); (v) the prediction that published user latents encode style (C3) failed (R² ≈ 0); (vi) the hybrid's non-inferiority to DFs (C4, second part) is formally not met (bound −0.014). Four baseline problems found during the study were fixed in favour of the baselines — a missing constant column in the low-rank latent, a scale non-identifiability in the probit group model, VPL's posterior collapse, and PAL-B's near-unregularised adaptation — and the runs before each fix are reported.

## 7. Limitations
Frozen-feature text heads for the main tables (a fine-tuned head in §5.4); three datasets, one language; PRISM scores binned to 5 levels; rater offsets are text-independent by design — interactions with content exist in principle and are what the low-rank latent can capture; we test linear decoders for the text-side measurement only; adaptation uses the rater's ratings of other items, not of the same item.

## Appendix A. Protocols and amendments
All analyses were specified in timestamped internal protocols (PROTOCOL_STAGE1, PROTOCOL_STAGE2, PROTOCOL_STAGE2B, PROTOCOL_STAGE2C); stages 2b and 2c were written after the stage-2a results. Changes after a result were logged as numbered amendments with the reason; the main ones: the first decoding split (a = b = 2) was structurally empty (two ratings determine their distribution) and was replaced by a = 3, b = 1; hand-set penalties in the rater–item model over-fitted and were replaced by inner cross-validation (which reversed an earlier, wrong conclusion that rater shifts do not help); penalty grids were extended when selections fell on grid edges, before any hypothesis was read; the low-rank baseline lacked a constant column and was fixed in its favour; the probit group model had a scale non-identifiability and was fixed; VPL suffered posterior collapse and its KL weight was re-selected including 0; PAL-B's adaptation ridge was re-selected by k-shot validation. Every superseded full run is kept and reported in the supplementary results; two debugging smoke runs and one fold whose output was deleted unread when the penalty grids were extended are not.

## Appendix B. Hypotheses and outcomes

## Appendix C. Implementation
Text features: last-token final-layer states of Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct under a 5-point judge prompt (MultiPref, PRISM) or a 3-level safety prompt (DICES), label-free PCA to 256 dimensions. Folds: 5 rater folds (stratified by group) × 5 prompt-group folds; validation: 15 % of training prompt groups. k-shot draws: 5 per k, identical across all models (RC-LoRA: draw 0 only). PRISM item folds are by conversation. Bootstrap: rater clusters, B = 2,000. LoRA: r = 16 on all attention and MLP projections, lr $10^{-4}$, one epoch, accumulation 8. VPL / PAL-B: official architectures (latent 512, hidden 512; d_pref 512, mlp2-gelu projectors, angle learner) with an ordered-logistic link for graded labels; VPL's KL weight selected from {0.01 (official), 0.001, 0} and PAL-B's adaptation ridge by k-shot validation (non-official choices made to prevent collapse and over-fitting).
