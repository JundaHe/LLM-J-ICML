## Abstract (draft)
Personalisation gains can reflect improved rating calibration, predictable differences between users, or better choices than a population model. We evaluate these separately using held-out users and prompts, few-shot adaptation, population baselines and exchanged-parameter controls. Across three preference and safety datasets, text-independent rater offsets improve graded-label log-likelihood by 0.08–0.10 nats per rating from twenty ratings, while the evaluated implementations of published methods show small direction or choice gains on MultiPref and native PRISM choices. On Community Alignment, twenty support choices from other prompts predict which of two disagreeing users selected which response on identical four-response items: assignment accuracy is 62.2 % [58.6, 65.6], against 50 % chance. A predictor using response positions alone is also informative. On the shared-item panel, isotropic adaptation gains 0.70 pairwise accuracy points over the population model, although the user-by-item interval includes zero; log-likelihood improves under both bootstrap analyses. The frozen procedure does not establish benefit on MultiPref. Changing the population-training procedure shifts measured gains by about 0.6 points. These findings motivate separate evaluations of rating calibration, user-associated choice predictability and predictive benefit over a population baseline.

## 1. Introduction
Reward models and LLM judges are trained on human ratings that disagree. A recent line of work treats this disagreement as signal to be modelled rather than noise to be averaged: distributional preference learning estimates a distribution over each alternative's utility rather than a point score (Siththaranjan et al., 2024); jury learning predicts individual annotators from content, annotator and demographic-group embeddings (Gordon et al., 2022), and large datasets such as PRISM are analysed by sociodemographic group (Kirk et al., 2024); personalised reward models learn a per-user latent, inferred for a new user from a few examples (Poddar et al., 2024; Chen et al., 2025). The pluralistic-alignment agenda (Sorensen et al., 2024) makes the representation of individual and group differences a first-class goal.

What these models learn about a person is rarely tested directly. Evaluations typically use synthetic users whose preferences differ by construction, binary preference labels, or users seen in training. We ask a simple question under a common protocol on real raters: **given k ratings from someone the model has never seen, how much better can it predict their other ratings — in particular, which response they prefer — and what is it that it learns?** We find that three outcomes usually reported together come apart: the largest few-shot gains on graded ratings are captured by text-independent scale-use offsets; users' choices among identical alternatives are predictable from their other choices; and improvements in choice over a competent population model are small, dataset-dependent and sensitive to how that population model is trained. Semi-synthetic controls show what content preferences the evaluated methods could and could not have detected.

Measurement theory suggests a candidate answer that reward modelling largely ignores: raters differ in **how they use the response scale** — some say "clearly better" where others say "slightly better", some rarely call a tie, some are harsher across the board (Linacre, 1989; Baumgartner & Steenkamp, 2001). Such response styles are text-independent, cheap to estimate, and invisible to models that condition only on content or demographics. We make them a reference point for calibration. Because they are text-independent they barely change which response a rater is predicted to prefer (only through asymmetric "clearly"/"slightly" mass); a personalised reward model that learns content preferences should therefore show its value in ranking, and a model whose likelihood gain does not exceed the scale-use offsets has not shown that it learns more than scale use.

**Contributions.** (1) **Graded-rating gains can be scale use.** Under a new-rater protocol (held-out raters and prompts, k-shot adaptation, population and exchanged-parameter controls), a few text-independent rater offsets capture the largest demonstrated few-shot gains on three preference and safety datasets and two code-readability studies; they improve graded-label likelihood, not which response is preferred, and vanish under binary labels (§4–5). (2) **User differences need their own test — ideally on identical items.** On Community Alignment, where many users judged the same prompt with the same four responses, support histories predict which user chooses which response among identical alternatives (62 % assignment accuracy between disagreeing users against 50 %); attribute-derived scores remain predictive when attributes and response positions are fitted jointly, and a position-only predictor is also informative (§6). (3) **Beneficial personalisation needs a competent population baseline.** Own-versus-other contrasts do not measure benefit; gains over the population model are small (+0.3 to +0.7 choice points where established), not established on MultiPref, and the measured gain changes by ~0.6 points with the population-training procedure alone (§6). Semi-synthetic positive controls bound what the evaluated learners could have detected (§5.6); a screened attribute learner and the covariance-calibrated procedure are reported as supporting tools (Appendix G, F). Protocols, amendments and failed hypotheses are in Appendices A, B, E and F.

## 2. Related work
**Pluralistic and distributional reward models.** Distributional preference learning (Siththaranjan et al., 2024), diverging-preference detection (Zhang et al., 2025), PRISM (Kirk et al., 2024), MultiPref (Miranda et al., 2025) and DICES (Aroyo et al., 2023) motivate modelling disagreement; jury learning (Gordon et al., 2022) and multi-annotator models (Davani et al., 2022) predict individual annotators, as do disagreement deconvolution (Gordon et al., 2021), per-rater toxicity models (Kumar et al., 2021), demographic and opinion-based prediction of unseen annotators (Fleisig et al., 2023) and multilevel models of DICES raters (Homan et al., 2024). Graded preference strength enters reward training as a margin (Touvron et al., 2023; Wang et al., 2025), and ties have classical paired-comparison models (Rao & Kupper, 1967; Davidson, 1970). **Personalised reward models** infer a user latent from a few examples (Poddar et al., 2024; Chen et al., 2025), represent users in a low-rank reward subspace (Bose et al., 2025), induce persona prompts from a user's history (Ryan et al., 2025, who also release a benchmark of PRISM and Chatbot Arena users), learn per-user embeddings jointly with the reward model (Li et al., 2024), fit a new user as a combination of shared base rewards (Shenfeld et al., 2025; Luo et al., 2025), or infer the user with neural processes (Kobalczyk et al., 2024); related work personalises the policy (Jang et al., 2023; Feng et al., 2024), adapts to groups (Zhao et al., 2024; Chakraborty et al., 2024) or studies personalisation versus aggregation (Park et al., 2024). Value profiles condition a rating decoder on a rater's demonstrations and are tested on held-out raters (Sorensen et al., 2025). Benchmarks often simulate users by personas or mixtures of reward models (Castricato et al., 2025; Zollo et al., 2025); real multi-rater data with deliberately diverse candidates are emerging (Zhang et al., 2026). VPL's latent can collapse under sparse preference data (Kim & Kim, 2026). A multi-faceted comparison of personalisation methods (Dong et al., 2025) reports large differences between methods and safety costs. Annotator embeddings (Deng et al., 2023) and per-annotator heads (Davani et al., 2022) are close relatives of our offsets; our contribution is not the offset model but the new-rater test and what it shows. Rater groups have been found to use safety severity scales differently (Mishra et al., 2026), which is consistent with the leniency component we find on DICES. **Annotator demographics** as predictors of labels have been found weak in several studies (e.g. Orlikowski et al., 2023). **Measurement theory**: rater severity and scale use are standard facets in many-facet Rasch measurement (Linacre, 1989; Eckes, 2011) and response styles are a classical survey-methods topic (Baumgartner & Steenkamp, 2001). We connect the last line to the first: for new-rater prediction, the measurement-theory rater facet is what the reward model is missing. **Collaborative filtering and annotator models.** User and item bias terms carry much of the predictive gain in collaborative filtering (Koren et al., 2009), and user-specific ordinal thresholds predict personal rating distributions (Koren & Sill, 2011); annotator models estimate rater-specific labelling behaviour (Dawid & Skene, 1979; Raykar et al., 2010; Hovy et al., 2013), and the label-variation literature argues for modelling it (Plank, 2022; Uma et al., 2021). Our offsets are of this kind and are not new; what is new is testing personalised reward models against them on new raters, and measuring what content personalisation these models could detect at all.

## 3. Data, and where the predictable structure sits
**Data.** MultiPref (Miranda et al., 2025; 10,461 comparisons; 4 ratings each by 2 crowd and 2 expert raters; 227 persistent raters; 5 ordered levels A-clearly … B-clearly). PRISM (Kirk et al., 2024; 68,371 responses each rated 1–100 by one of 1,396 users; binned to 5 fixed levels). DICES-350 (Aroyo et al., 2023; 350 conversations fully crossed with 104 quality-checked raters; safe / unsure / unsafe).

**A learner-free measurement (Appendix D).** Before fitting any personalised model we measured, on MultiPref, where held-out predictable structure sits: beyond item location, rater-specific thresholds improve held-out likelihood about 25 times more than item-specific dispersion (+0.036 vs +0.0014 nats per rating), the item-level shape of the rating distribution that replicates across disjoint rater panels is small (split-half reliability 0.25), and about a tenth of it is decodable from text. Beyond item location, the predictable structure is therefore mostly rater-side scale use, while the item-level shape that replicates across disjoint panels is small and only partly in the text. These two facts come from different estimands and do not say that disagreement is "not in the text". The stage-1 decision rule we had written down returned **"mixed"**, because G/X was not ≤ 10 % (interval 5.7–11.9 %); the factorised model of §4 was chosen with that outcome known.

## 4. A three-parameter scale-use baseline
(Figure 2 illustrates the effect of the offsets.) Let ℓ(x) ∈ R^K be the class logits of a text-only distributional reward model for item x (K ordered rating levels). For rater r we add a text-independent offset vector: p(y | x, r) = softmax(ℓ(x) + b_r). Variants: **DF** (b_r ∈ R^K, free), **DFs** (two-sided preference scales only: b_r tied symmetrically, (e, s, t, s, e) — extreme, slight, tie — 3 parameters, of which 2 are identifiable because the softmax is invariant to a common shift; no A-vs-B position term). Rater offsets have a Gaussian prior centred on the training-rater mean; a new rater's offsets are the MAP estimate from k of their ratings with the text model frozen. Baselines under the same protocol: **D** (text-only distributional RM), **DG** (group offsets: crowd/expert, race, region), **DL** (low-rank text-interacting latent: ℓ(x) + (Wx + w₀)·z_r, d = 8, the personalised-RM family). An interpretable probit family (rater shift / log-scale on an ordered-probit text head) is reported as a secondary analysis.

**Training and adaptation.** The text model and the training raters' offsets are fitted jointly by penalised maximum likelihood,
$$\min \sum_{(i,r,y)} -\log p(y \mid x_i, r) + \lambda_{\text{text}} \|V\|^2 + \lambda_r \sum_r \|b_r\|^2,$$
with $\lambda_{\text{text}}$ chosen on the text-only model and $\lambda_r$ on held-out items of the training raters. A new rater $r^*$ is adapted from $k$ ratings $\{(x_j, y_j)\}$ by $\hat b_{r^*} = \arg\min_b \sum_j -\log \mathrm{softmax}(\ell(x_j) + b)_{y_j} + \lambda_r \|b - \bar b\|^2$, with $\bar b$ the training-rater mean (the prior mean; $\hat b = \bar b$ at $k = 0$) and the text model frozen — a convex problem in 3–5 parameters, solved in milliseconds. Any text model that outputs graded log-probabilities can be used: we use a ridge head on frozen LLM features (§5.1–5.2), a LoRA-fine-tuned LLM reward model (§5.3) and the decoder of a published personalised reward model (§5.4).

## 5. Experiments
**Protocol.** 5 folds; in each, 20 % of raters (stratified by group) and 20 % of prompt groups are held out; models are trained on training raters × training items with penalties chosen on a validation split; each test rater is adapted on k ∈ {0, 5, 10, 20, 50} of their ratings of *training* items (5 random draws) and evaluated on their ratings of *held-out* items. Primary metric: held-out log-likelihood per rating; also RPS and preference-direction accuracy; rater-cluster bootstrap (B = 2,000). Because test raters share test items on MultiPref and DICES, the headline k = 20 contrasts were also re-estimated with a two-way bootstrap that resamples raters and prompt groups independently: on graded labels every log-likelihood interval widens by at most 0.007 on either side and no log-likelihood conclusion changes; direction-accuracy differences of about a point or less (the style model's +0.5 on MultiPref, VPL's direction advantage over the style model) lose significance and are marked where they appear. Text model in §5.1–5.2: frozen Qwen2.5-7B features with a ridge head; §5.3: a LoRA-fine-tuned reward model.

### 5.3 On a fine-tuned reward model
With a LoRA-fine-tuned Qwen2.5-7B reward model as the text model, the three offsets still add +0.100 [+0.071, +0.130] nats per rating at k = 20 — as on the frozen-feature head — and the gains of fine-tuning and of personalisation add. Against in-context personalisation (a LoRA model given the rater's k ratings in the prompt, three seeds per fold) the offsets are better by +0.041 [+0.027, +0.054] nats and +1.9 [+0.9, +2.7] direction points (Appendix B).

### 5.4 Published personalised reward models
We reimplemented VPL (Poddar et al., 2024) and PAL-B (Chen et al., 2025) from their official code as heads on the same frozen per-response embeddings and evaluated them under the same new-rater protocol, refitting the text-only and style models on those embeddings. On graded labels, at k = 20 the style model is better than VPL by +0.092 [+0.068, +0.119] nats per rating ([+0.063, +0.124] when prompt groups are also resampled) and than PAL-B by +0.158. VPL's latent does carry rater information once its posterior collapse is prevented (KL weight selected as 0 or 0.001; +0.087 over its own unpersonalised version), but only lifts it to the level of the text-only model; PAL-B's few-shot user weights give no gain. Their user representations are unrelated to response style (cross-validated R² ≈ 0 against the style offsets). On binary labels — the setting in which both methods were proposed — every method's personalisation gain is at most 0.002 nats (small but nonzero for PAL-B with K = 4, and for VPL and the style model at k = 50), and VPL's text head is the best binary model (the style model is 0.010 nats behind it and 0.005 behind the text-only model, differences of the size of item-sampling noise): on these panels the largest demonstrated gains are captured by calibration of graded levels and ties, which binary preference data discard. VPL's per-response reward head is slightly better at predicting *which* response is preferred (0.677 vs 0.668), an effect present without personalisation and not significant once prompt groups are also resampled (style model − VPL: −0.9 points [−2.7, +0.8]). The offsets are also a plug-in: added to VPL (fitted on the same k ratings, VPL frozen) they improve it by +0.088 [+0.066, +0.111] nats per rating at k = 20, tie with the style model on likelihood (−0.004 [−0.014, +0.005]) and keep VPL's direction accuracy (+1.1 points over the style model alone; rater bootstrap only).

### 5.6 Could these methods have detected content personalisation? A semi-synthetic positive control
A ranking null is informative only if the methods would detect content-dependent preferences where they exist. We keep MultiPref's structure (raters, items, who rated what, folds and k-shot draws) and replace the labels by semi-synthetic ones. A generator — the text-only head plus scale-use offsets, fitted on all ratings — gives each rating a distribution, to which a rater-specific content term $(\theta_r^\top g_i)\,(-2,-1,0,1,2)$ is added to the logits; $g_i$ are two features of the response pair (the log length ratio and a random direction in the per-response embedding space, orthogonal to the consensus quality direction) and $\theta_r \sim N(0, \tau^2 I)$. The scale $\tau$ is calibrated on the generator alone so that an oracle that knows $\theta_r$ gains +1.7, +5.2, +10.1 or +21.1 points of direction accuracy on the test ratings; $\tau = 0$ is a null control. Every method is then run under the paper's protocol. Because a method whose unpersonalised state is not the population state can improve with anyone's ratings, the contrast is **the method adapted on the rater's own k ratings minus the same method given another test rater's adapted parameters** (a random derangement within fold, k and draw). Before the runs we fixed that the protocol has power at a strength if some content-interacting method has a lower 95 % bound above +1 point at k = 20; the +10 and +21 panels and the untuned-prior low-rank runs were added after the first results and are exploratory.

### 5.7 How much room is there? Conflicts among aspect judgements are rare
Every MultiPref annotation carries the annotator's own helpfulness, truthfulness and harmlessness preferences, so a rater's trade-off between these values is observable. With the rater's own aspect labels as inputs (an oracle for the content side), a pooled trade-off already gets 97.5 % of directions right, and a rater-specific trade-off fitted on all of the rater's training ratings adds only +0.10 [−0.24, +0.43] points (+0.44 even in-sample). The three recorded aspect judgements point in opposite directions in only 4.0 % of held-out non-tie comparisons; there individual trade-offs matter (in-sample +9.7 points), but they are too rare to move accuracy. A calibrated test with the item's consensus aspects from other annotators finds individual weights transferring worse than the population's (−0.28 [−0.56, −0.06] at k = 20; exploratory, Appendix B).

### 5.8 A learner that finds the axes
A screened empirical-Bayes learner over 24 interpretable attribute differences (SAP) recovers the semi-synthetic content preferences that the published learners miss, with near-zero effects on null panels, and abstains on real MultiPref under a prospectively specified rule (+0.28 [−0.00, +0.58] points at k = 20). Details: Appendix G.

### 5.9 Developers rating code readability
Two code-readability studies have persistent developer identities: Buse–Weimer (121 raters × 100 Java snippets) and Dorn (3,393 developers with at least ten ratings of 360 Java/Python/CUDA snippets). With Qwen2.5-Coder-7B features, scale use transfers to new developers: offsets gain +0.054 [+0.036, +0.074] nats per rating at k = 20 (Buse–Weimer) and +0.040 [+0.031, +0.049] at k = 10 (Dorn). For content we measure within-developer concordance of predicted and given ratings over snippet pairs, comparing a developer's own weights with population weights under the same offsets. SAP with 22 classical readability attributes (line and identifier length, indentation, nesting, comments, operators, …) gives +0.40 [−0.25, +1.06] points at the pre-set k = 20 on Buse–Weimer (+1.30 [+0.46, +2.18] at k = 50, exploratory) and −0.19 [−0.81, +0.42] on Dorn; the low-rank latent is inconclusive on Buse–Weimer and switched off by validation on Dorn. Details, the pre-set rule's literal outcome on Dorn (met through the own − other contrast, which is not on the oracle scale) and permutation controls are in Appendices A–B.

## 6. What personalisation buys: graded-rating prediction, user signal, and small choice gains
**Binary labels remove the signal on MultiPref.** On MultiPref with ties dropped and labels binarised, every method's personalisation gain is at most 0.002 nats per rating (§5.4), and the scale-use baseline's binary accuracy equals the text-only model's (−0.0002). On these graded panels the largest demonstrated gains concern graded levels and ties — which binary preference data, the format in which most personalised reward models are trained and evaluated, discard (Community Alignment shows that binary choices can also carry predictable user differences, §6).
**Ranking moves little.** Text-independent offsets change how strongly a rater is predicted to prefer a response, not which response: on MultiPref the scale-use baseline raises direction accuracy by only +0.005 [+0.001, +0.008] at k = 20 (about half of it already at k = 0), while RPS drops from 0.1593 to 0.1532. On single-response safety ratings the same offsets do move the safe/unsafe decision (+4.8 points on DICES), because there a rater's threshold *is* the decision. Text-only direction accuracy on MultiPref (0.674) is already as high as the agreement in direction between two human raters of the same item (0.673 [0.666, 0.680]): a population model predicts a new rater about as well as another person does. **Seen raters.** With all their training ratings (median 134), training raters' own parameters beat another rater's by −0.11 [−0.57, +0.36] direction points for the low-rank latent (offsets +0.10, −0.23): the ranking null is not specific to few-shot adaptation (exploratory, Appendix B). Against its own k = 0 version, no method's direction accuracy on MultiPref rises by more than 0.2 points at k = 20, and every upper 95 % bound stays below +0.8 points even when prompt groups are resampled together with raters (VPL +0.07 [−0.57, +0.74], style model −0.10 [−0.69, +0.54], PAL-B −0.48 [−1.11, +0.14]).
**The ranking null replicates on real users' own choices.** PRISM users each saw 2–4 responses to their own prompt, scored them and chose one; pairing the chosen response with every other one gives 41,199 native pairwise choices of 1,396 users. Under the same new-user protocol (5 user folds × 5 conversation folds, k of the user's pairs from training conversations), we fixed before any result that the ranking null would be stated only if every method's improvement in choice accuracy over its own k = 0 version had an upper 95 % bound below one point at k = 20. It holds for all of them (1,033 new users at k = 0; the 669 with at least 20 adaptation pairs at k = 20): VPL +0.12 points [−0.08, +0.33], PAL-B +0.0 [−0.4, +0.4] (K = 2) and +0.3 [−0.1, +0.7] (K = 4), the 5-offset model −0.6 [−1.4, +0.2]; the symmetric scale-use model is identical to the text-only model on binary choices, because a binary label carries no intensity. LoRe (Bose et al., 2025), which reports +10 points for unseen PRISM users, run here with its own few-shot fit and with a validated adaptation ridge under the same pre-set rule, also fails it: own gain at k = 20 −1.5 [−2.5, −0.4] (regularised −0.5 [−1.2, +0.2]), own − other +0.2 [−0.8, +1.2]. In LoRe's own setting (its embeddings, split and pair construction) we obtain 67.4 % for the monolithic model and 63.6 % for LoRe (published 61.0 % and 71.0 %; Appendix B): the published gain is not reproduced. With a graded label cut from the users' own score differences (thresholds fixed in advance), the MultiPref pattern returns at half the size (scale-use model +0.043 [+0.031, +0.057] nats per rating at k = 20, VPL +0.015, PAL-B 0.000; no direction gain above +0.2 points).
**Community Alignment: who chooses what on identical items, and what adaptation buys.** Community Alignment (CA; first turns; 2,957 new users in five countries and languages; each choice event's chosen response paired with each of the other three). Accuracy below is pairwise winner-versus-other accuracy, not four-way winner selection. All CA analyses below were specified before their outcomes but use data examined in earlier stages (Appendix F).

*Identity on identical items (Stage 13, frozen).* 55 % of first-turn conversations use pre-generated prompts whose four candidate responses are identical across users (2,928 items, median 15 users, one language per item, fixed order). For two users of the same item who chose different responses a and b, the population utilities cancel and the adapted models predict the assignment from S = (δ_u − δ_v)ᵀ(x_a − x_b), with model-implied probability σ(S). With validated isotropic adaptation over length, paragraphs and list/heading lines (k = 20 support events on other prompts; the Stage-12 procedure unchanged), the **conditional assignment log score gains +0.066 [+0.034, +0.091] nats over chance and assignment accuracy is 62.2 % [58.6, 65.6]** (4,608 disagreeing pairs from 1,336 users on 1,116 items; item × user bootstrap). The estimate is unchanged with uncentred features, the bootstrap is centred, positive-multiplier and prompt-clustered intervals are [+0.046, +0.083] and [+0.036, +0.093], and every fold is positive (0.042–0.082). The estimand is narrow: given two disagreeing users, their support choices and the two responses they picked, assign the responses; it conditions on the fitted deviations and population model.

*Position.* The four fixed positions differ systematically in these attributes (label-blind check), so we refitted with position indicators added (after the result). The attribute-derived part of the jointly fitted scores keeps +0.060 [+0.032, +0.084] nats, a position-only predictor is also informative (63.8 %), and the joint model's paired gain over position-only is +0.026 [−0.000, +0.046]. With correlated predictors under one prior this does not separate content from position: we claim user-specific choice predictability on identical alternatives, not a content preference, and not a psychological position habit.

*Benefit over the population model.* On all test events of these items (2,383 items, 1,436 users; population accuracy 72.4 %), isotropic adaptation gains **+0.70 [+0.24, +1.14] pairwise accuracy points and +0.018 [+0.014, +0.023] nats** — the pre-set user-bootstrap rule for benefit is met, but the user × item interval for accuracy covers 0 ([−0.07, +1.43]) while log-likelihood improves under both, so an accuracy benefit that generalises across items is unresolved; nor is the gain shown to be below one point. Evaluated frozen on the same users' unanalysed second turns (procedure refitted there; Stage 12), the covariance-calibrated variant gains +0.38 [+0.13, +0.64] points and +0.0046 nats and isotropic +0.31 [+0.05, +0.58]; the prompt × user interval covers 0. On MultiPref the frozen isotropic procedure did not establish improvement (−0.28 [−0.64, +0.08] points, 202 held-out annotators). On CA the estimated covariance shows no advantage over isotropic shrinkage: on these identical items TRV − isotropic is −0.10 [−0.28, +0.07] points and −0.003 [−0.005, −0.001] nats (isotropic better in log-likelihood), on all first turns −0.17 [−0.29, −0.05] points, on second turns +0.07 [−0.07, +0.22]. On MultiPref the pre-set covariance-advantage rule is met under the user bootstrap (+0.23 [+0.04, +0.42]) only because isotropic adaptation falls slightly below μ while the covariance prior stays at μ, and the two-way interval covers 0 ([−0.12, +0.57]); we do not read it as a covariance benefit. The validated scales imply strong shrinkage, consistent with event-level model-implied achievable gains (+1.9 points under a Gaussian model, +0.9 under t₃) far above the realised ones.

*The measured gain depends on the population baseline.* On 1,799 common users, one-dimensional length adaptation gains +0.88 / +1.08 points (random / prompt-separated test events) against a population model fitted on low-volume annotators, as in WIMHF's protocol, and +0.26 / +0.47 against one fitted on all other-fold users: the population-procedure effect is +0.62 [+0.41, +0.81] and +0.61 [+0.30, +0.93] (paired), while the prompt-split contrast is inconclusive (+0.20 [−0.20, +0.59]). The two procedures differ in volume, composition and prior estimation, so no mechanism is isolated. (Our line-count proxy is not WIMHF's discovered feature; with it, list/heading adaptation does not beat the population coefficient — a related protocol study, not a failed reproduction.)

*Earlier evidence.* Before the identical-item analysis, own adapted parameters already beat another user's for four of six evaluated methods, surviving Holm adjustment (LoRe-reg, VPL, PAL4 and LoRe: +3.27 [+2.85, +3.70] although its adaptation lowers its own accuracy by 2.34 points; within-country +3.22; DFp and PAL2 intervals cover 0), and users' length deviations reproduced across disjoint prompt sets; own − other compares two adapted models and does not measure benefit, and task mix could create it. These analyses, the covariance-prior development and their multiplicity sensitivity are in Appendix F; the identical-item test supersedes them as evidence of user-specific choice predictability.
## 7. Recommendations
(i) **Separate three outcomes.** Report graded-rating likelihood (calibration and resolution), a test of user-specific choice predictability, and choice or ranking gains over the population model as different results; a gain on one does not imply the others. (ii) **Test user differences on identical items** where the data allow it (an assignment test between disagreeing users, as in §6): own-versus-other contrasts on different prompts confound user differences with task mix and do not measure benefit. (iii) **Report a competent population baseline and its training procedure.** The measured personalisation gain changed by about 0.6 points with the population model's training users alone (§6). (iv) **Report a scale-use baseline** (a few text-independent offsets per rater) next to any personalised reward model on graded labels, and evaluate content adaptation with those offsets retained. (v) **Evaluate on raters unseen in training and on held-out prompts**, with a permutation control and a positive control that shows what the learner could have detected. (vi) Keep graded labels and ties when individual differences matter; sample comparisons where value conflicts occur (4 % of MultiPref comparisons).

## 8. Discussion
**What do personalisation gains measure?** In our evaluations three things that are often reported as one come apart. Graded-rating gains for new raters are largely scale use: text-independent offsets capture them, and the evaluated personalised models' representations do not encode it (R² ≈ 0; adding offsets to VPL recovers the whole gain, +0.088). User-specific choice predictability is real where the data allow a clean test: on identical CA items, support choices predict which of two disagreeing users chose which response (62 %), although we cannot attribute this to content rather than position. Choice gains over a competent population model are small and fragile: below one point and positive under the primary rule on CA turn 2, positive under the user bootstrap but unresolved across items on the identical-item panel, not established on MultiPref, and sensitive to how the population model is trained.

**Why did published personalised models learn little about new raters here?** Published evaluations differ in user populations, label formats, representations and splits: synthetic users whose preferences differ by construction, users seen in training, or population models fitted on a different user group. Our 2 × 2 on CA shows that the last choice alone changes the measured gain by about 0.6 points; our unresolved native-reproduction discrepancies (LoRe, and WIMHF's discovered feature, which we did not re-implement) prevent attributing the remaining differences to evaluation design. All method conclusions concern the evaluated implementations.

## 9. Limitations and analysis log
Protocols, amendments and failed hypotheses: Appendices A, B, E. The main tables use frozen-feature text heads (a LoRA reward model only in §5.3), and compressed features make the published methods weaker detectors than full features. Our VPL, PAL and LoRe are reimplementations as heads on frozen embeddings; VPL's and PAL's published numbers were not reproduced in their native settings, and LoRe's PRISM gain is not reproduced even in its own setting with its embeddings, for reasons we could not isolate. The positive control injects low-dimensional preferences on MultiPref only, and its panels are noisier than the real data, so detection limits transfer only approximately. The aspect analyses rest on MultiPref's concurrent aspect labels. SAP selects among supplied attributes and does not discover new ones; its dictionary contains the attributes of the semantic injection, and its screening step was amended after the first runs (Appendix A). The first three datasets are English; Community Alignment spans five languages, which the phrase-based attributes cover only for English; and the per-rater calibration is not shown to change a downstream decision. The CA analyses use data examined in earlier stages (the Stage-12 and -13 tests are prospectively specified but not on untouched data); the identical-item test conditions on fitted models; features omit conversation history except in the third-turn test; and the identity signal is not separated from response position.

## Impact Statement
This paper evaluates personalised reward models, which are meant to let language models serve people whose preferences differ. It finds that reported personalisation gains can reflect rating-scale use, user-associated choice predictability or real improvements over a population model, and that these need separate tests: gains on graded ratings were largely scale use, users' choices on identical alternatives were predictable from their other choices, and improvements over a population model were small and dataset-dependent. Predicting individuals' choices also raises privacy and manipulation concerns that a deployment would need to address; we report no individual-level results. The datasets are public, were collected with consent by their creators, and are used under their licences; rater identifiers are used only as anonymous indices.

**Scale use and the shared reward model.** Training the text model jointly with rater offsets and then using the population-mean offsets (k = 0) does not give a better consensus model than ordinary training: −0.003 [−0.010, +0.003] nats on MultiPref (direction +0.3 points, n.s.), −0.022 [−0.037, −0.008] on DICES, +0.001 on PRISM, −0.009 [−0.016, −0.004] with the LoRA reward model. Ordinary training averages scale use out; it matters for predicting a person, not for learning the content preference.

**What the offsets encode.** In the probit family, rater *scale* matters more than rater *shift* on MultiPref (+0.036 [+0.020, +0.057] at k = 20), but rater *shift* dominates on DICES (−0.053) and PRISM (−0.031): leniency/strictness on single-response rating scales, intensity on two-sided preference scales.

**What this does and does not say about pluralism.** Our results do not show that people agree about content: the item-level disagreement that replicates across rater panels is real (split-half reliability 0.25 on MultiPref) and about a tenth of it is decodable from text. They show that, for predicting an individual new rater from few ratings, that content-level disagreement is a small part of what is predictable, and that demographic or crowd/expert groups add nothing beyond the text.

**Fine-tuned and in-context reward models (§5.3, full text).** With a LoRA-fine-tuned Qwen2.5-7B reward model (D-LoRA, trained per fold on the training raters' rating distributions) as the text model, the three-number style model still adds +0.100 [+0.071, +0.130] nats per rating at k = 20 — the same as its gain on the frozen-feature head (+0.100). Fine-tuning improves the text-only model by +0.027 [+0.016, +0.039] (direction accuracy +1.4 points) and the style model by the same +0.027: the two gains add. Personalising the frozen-feature model with 20 ratings per rater gains more than fine-tuning the text model (+0.072 [+0.041, +0.106]). DFs keeps the direction accuracy of D-LoRA (+0.002 [−0.002, +0.006]). Against in-context personalisation — a separate LoRA model on the same base, trained with ten of the rater's other ratings in the prompt and evaluated with the new rater's k ratings in the prompt — the three offsets on D-LoRA are better at k = 20. The in-context model's result varies with its training seed (two of fifteen runs failed, gaining −0.078 and −0.055 over D-LoRA against +0.021 to +0.125 for the others; the two failed runs have the highest training loss), so it was trained with three seeds per fold, and we fixed in advance that "better" requires a lower bound above zero for every seed and for the seed average. Averaged over seeds — the expected result of one training run — the offsets are better by +0.041 [+0.027, +0.054] nats per rating (209 raters; per seed +0.062, +0.018 and +0.042, every lower bound at least +0.005) and more accurate on which response the rater prefers (+1.9 points [+0.9, +2.7]). **Value conflicts (§5.7, full text).** The positive control asks what the methods could detect; MultiPref also lets us ask how much there is to detect. Every MultiPref annotation carries the annotator's own helpfulness, truthfulness and harmlessness preferences next to the overall one, so a rater's way of trading these values off is observable. We fit an ordinal model of the overall preference from the annotator's own aspect labels (their values and absolute values) with rater-specific cut-points (scale use) and either a pooled trade-off or a rater-specific trade-off, adapted on k of the rater's training-item annotations and evaluated on held-out items **with the rater's own aspect labels as inputs** — an oracle for the content side. The pooled trade-off weights harmlessness most (6.1, five-fold mean), then helpfulness (4.0) and truthfulness (2.0). With the rater's own aspect labels as inputs the pooled model already gets 97.5 % of directions right (74 % on conflict items): aspect and overall labels are produced together by the same annotator, so this ceiling concerns a small residual and is a property of MultiPref's concurrent labelling.

## Appendix D. Where is the predictable structure? Estimators and results
**Estimators.** For an item with ratings on $K$ ordered levels, let $h$ be the empirical rating distribution and $C$ the cumulative operator. Split the item's raters at random into disjoint panels $P_1$ ($a$ raters) and $P_2$, and let $q$ be a cross-fitted *location-only* forecast with exactly the mode and mean of $P_1$ (built from other items in the same mode-and-mean cell). With $r_1 = C(h_1 - q)$, $r_2 = C(h_2 - q)$, $M = \|r_1\|^2/n$ and $X = \langle r_1, r_2\rangle/n$, the forecasting value of the raw distribution over its own location obeys the identity $S(h_1) - S(q) = M - 2X$ under the ranked probability score, so $\rho = X/M$ is a split-half reliability of the distribution's *shape* and the raw distribution beats its location exactly when $\rho > 1/2$. Text decodability is the proper-score gain $G = (2\langle \hat r, r_2\rangle - \|\hat r\|^2)/n$ of a residual $\hat r$ decoded from frozen text features, cross-fitted over prompt groups, reported as the fraction $G/X$ of the reproducible shape. The rater–item decomposition fits ordered-probit models $Y^*_{ir} = \theta_i + \delta_r + \sigma_i\sigma_r\varepsilon$ with item locations $\theta_i$, rater shifts $\delta_r$, rater scales $\sigma_r$ and item dispersions $\sigma_i$, compares held-out likelihood over random cells (every held-out rater and item appears in training), and chooses all penalties by inner cross-validation.

**Reproducibility.** With the split-half estimator defined above, the reliability of the rating distribution's shape beyond its exact mode and mean is ρ = 0.25 [0.22, 0.29] on MultiPref overall preference with three raters (helpful 0.29, harmless 0.21): the raw few-rater distribution forecasts a new rater worse than its own location (ρ < ½).
**Text decodability.** A cross-fitted ridge decoder on frozen Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct features, scored on an independent rater panel, recovers G/X = 10.5 % [5.7 %, 11.9 %] of the reproducible shape (within-cell permutation control ≤ 0; positive control 0.35) — comparable to the upper end for ordinal quality ratings (upper bounds 0.4–10 %) and below NLI (17–26 %).
**Rater vs item.** A long-format ordered-probit decomposition with real rater identities and penalties tuned by inner cross-validation: rater-specific thresholds improve held-out likelihood by +0.036 nats per rating over item location alone, item-specific dispersion by a further +0.0014 (ratio 25; 19–36 across the five folds); of the rater part, rater scale contributes +0.031 and rater shift +0.005.

The matched other is itself about 0.35 points worse than the population model (own − population +0.45), so these contrasts measure user association, not benefit; clusters control coarse task categories, not identical tasks or candidate responses, and we claim user-associated length signal rather than a task-independent individual preference. The diagnostic's simulation calibration was not met as originally specified (Appendix F).

*(ii) The measured gain depends on the population baseline.* In a WIMHF-style protocol study (population model on low-volume annotators, held-out high-volume annotators adapted on 16 events; our line-count proxy on the multilingual panel, not WIMHF's discovered SAE feature, so this is a related protocol study rather than a failed reproduction), list/heading adaptation does not beat the population coefficient and length gains +1.06 [+0.80, +1.33] points. On 1,799 common users, changing the population-training procedure from low-volume annotators to all other-fold users reduces the length gain by about 0.6 points under both splits (+0.88 → +0.26 with random events; +1.08 → +0.47 with held-out prompt groups), (paired differences +0.62 [+0.41, +0.81] and +0.61 [+0.30, +0.93]), while separating support and test prompts does not reduce the point estimates (paired +0.20 [−0.20, +0.59] and +0.21 [−0.16, +0.58]; a reduction of up to about 0.2 points is not excluded). Population-model choice therefore substantially affects the measured personalisation gain; W and O differ in training volume, composition and prior estimation, so we do not attribute the reduction to a specific mechanism, and the split contrast changes the evaluation examples as well as support selection (Appendix F).

*(ii′) On identical items, histories predict who chooses what (Stage 13, frozen before outcomes).* 55 % of CA first-turn conversations use pre-generated prompts whose four candidate responses are identical across users (2,928 items, median 15 users each, one language per item, fixed order). For two users of the same item who chose different responses a and b, the population terms cancel and the adapted models predict the actual assignment from S = (δ_u − δ_v)ᵀ(x_a − x_b) alone. With validated isotropic adaptation over length, paragraphs and list/heading (k = 20 support events from other prompts), the assignment log-score gain is **+0.066 [+0.034, +0.091] nats** and the assignment accuracy **62.2 % [58.6, 65.6]** (1,116 items, 4,608 user pairs; item × user two-way bootstrap). Because the four fixed positions differ systematically in these attributes, we refitted with position indicators added (after the result): the attribute-derived part of the jointly fitted scores keeps +0.060 [+0.032, +0.084] nats (61.5 %), and a predictor using positions alone is also informative (63.8 %). With correlated predictors under one prior this does not separate content from position; it shows that attribute-derived scores remain predictive after a joint fit. Against the position-only predictor, the joint model's paired gain is +0.026 [−0.000, +0.046] nats (positive under a multiplier bootstrap, [+0.009, +0.040]): additional value of the attributes over positions is not established, and we do not attribute the identity signal to content rather than position. The endpoint-A interval is robust to positive-multiplier and prompt-clustered resampling ([+0.046, +0.083], [+0.036, +0.093]) and positive in every fold (0.042–0.082 nats); it is conditional on the fitted deviations and population models. On all test events of these items (agreements included), isotropic adaptation beats the population model by **+0.70 [+0.24, +1.14] pairwise accuracy points and +0.018 nats**, meeting the pre-set user-bootstrap rule; the user × item interval for accuracy covers 0 while log-likelihood improves under both, so an accuracy benefit that generalises across items is unresolved, and the gain is not shown to be below one point (with position indicators +1.07 [+0.53, +1.66]). This identifies persistent user-specific choice predictability on identical displayed alternatives, not an intrinsic length preference. The frozen isotropic procedure did not establish improvement on MultiPref: −0.28 [−0.64, +0.08] accuracy points (202 held-out annotators).

*(iii) Validated adaptation gives small, reproducible choice gains; no advantage of the estimated covariance is established.* Adaptation over interpretable attributes with the attribute set and prior scale selected on pseudo-new training users (TRV: θ̂_u − μ = c Ĉ (I + c I_u Ĉ)⁻¹ s_u, Ĉ the cross-half moment over training users) was developed on first turns after inspecting test results (+0.49 [+0.20, +0.77] points, +0.0088 nats; exploratory). Frozen and evaluated on the same users' **second turns** (83,238 never-analysed choices, 1,702 test users, k = 20 events), it gains **+0.38 [+0.13, +0.64] points and +0.0046 [+0.0031, +0.0063] nats per pair** over μ: by the pre-set rules a reproducible but sub-threshold benefit (upper bound below +1). The prompt-group × user bootstrap interval for accuracy includes 0 ([−0.04, +0.83]); no language is significant alone. Validated isotropic shrinkage over the same candidate attributes gains +0.31 [+0.05, +0.58]; TRV − isotropic +0.07 [−0.07, +0.22] (on first turns the nominal paired interval favoured isotropic, −0.17 [−0.29, −0.05]), so no advantage of the estimated covariance is established; in folds where length alone is selected the covariance is a scalar prior variance. The tuned rank-2 model selects no adaptation in three of five folds. The validated scales (c = 0.125–1, τ² = 0.03–0.3) mean strong shrinkage — light adaptation — consistent with model-implied achievable gains for this learner (event-level simulation, one winner per choice event: +1.9 points under a Gaussian model, +0.9 under t₃, ≈ 0 under a 10 % mixture) far above what is realised, and with I_u⁻¹ understating the sampling noise of dependent winner-versus-other pairs. These are new prompts and turns of previously studied users, with features that omit conversation history; the stage freezes the procedure and refits it, and does not show that first-turn user coefficients transfer. The one-point threshold is our pre-specified benchmark, not a universal boundary of useful personalisation.
