\begin{abstract}
Personalisation gains can reflect improved rating calibration, predictable differences between users, or better choices than a population model. We evaluate these separately using held-out users and prompts, few-shot adaptation, population baselines and exchanged-parameter controls. Across three preference and safety datasets, text-independent rater offsets improve graded-label log-likelihood by 0.08--0.10 nats per rating from twenty ratings, while our compressed-feature reimplementations of published methods show small direction or choice gains on MultiPref and native PRISM choices. On Community Alignment, twenty support choices from other prompts predict which of two disagreeing users selected which response on identical four-response items: assignment accuracy is 62.2 \% {[}58.6, 65.6{]}, against 50 \% chance. A predictor using response positions alone is also informative, and we do not separate content from position. On the shared-item panel, isotropic adaptation gains 0.70 pairwise accuracy points over the population model, although the user-by-item interval includes zero; log-likelihood improves under both bootstrap analyses. The frozen procedure does not establish benefit on MultiPref, and frozen first-turn deviations improve accuracy on users' later, disjoint conversations under user clustering but do not meet the prespecified joint accuracy-and-likelihood criterion over a history-conditioned population model. Changing the population-training procedure shifts measured gains by about 0.6 points. These findings motivate separate evaluations of rating calibration, user-associated choice predictability and predictive benefit over a population baseline.
\end{abstract}
\section{Introduction}

Reward models and LLM judges are trained on human ratings that disagree, and a recent line of work treats this disagreement as signal: distributional preference learning (\citealp{siththaranjan2024dpl}), jury learning (\citealp{gordon2022jury}) and personalised reward models that infer a per-user latent from a few examples (\citealp{poddar2024vpl}; \citealp{chen2025pal}), within the pluralistic-alignment agenda (\citealp{sorensen2024roadmap}).

What these models learn about a person is rarely tested directly. Evaluations typically use synthetic users whose preferences differ by construction, binary preference labels, or users seen in training. We ask a simple question under a common protocol on real raters: \textbf{given k ratings from someone the model has never seen, how much better can it predict their other ratings --- in particular, which response they prefer --- and what is it that it learns?} We find that three outcomes usually reported together come apart: the largest few-shot gains on graded ratings are captured by text-independent scale-use offsets; users' choices among identical alternatives are predictable from their other choices; and improvements in choice over a competent population model are small, dataset-dependent and sensitive to how that population model is trained. Semi-synthetic controls characterise which injected content-preference families the evaluated methods recover.

Measurement theory suggests a candidate answer that reward modelling largely ignores: raters differ in \textbf{how they use the response scale} --- some say ``clearly better'' where others say ``slightly better'', some rarely call a tie, some are harsher across the board (\citealp{linacre1989mfrm}; \citealp{baumgartner2001response}). Such response styles are text-independent, cheap to estimate, and invisible to models that condition only on content or demographics. We make them a reference point for calibration. On two-sided preference scales, symmetric offsets mainly model intensity and ties and cancel exactly for binary A/B labels at a fixed text head; unrestricted offsets can also change predicted decisions. Likelihood gains alone therefore do not establish content-dependent personalisation, and content adaptation should be evaluated with rater offsets retained.

\textbf{Contributions.} (1) \textbf{Graded-rating gains can be scale use.} Under a new-rater protocol (held-out raters and prompts, k-shot adaptation, population and exchanged-parameter controls), a few text-independent rater offsets capture the largest demonstrated few-shot gains on three preference and safety datasets and two code-readability studies; in their symmetric form they chiefly improve graded-label likelihood, change direction on graded MultiPref only slightly, and cancel exactly under binary labels (\S{}4--5). (2) \textbf{User differences need their own test --- ideally on identical items.} On Community Alignment, where many users judged the same prompt with the same four responses, support histories predict which user chooses which response among identical alternatives (62 \% assignment accuracy between disagreeing users against 50 \%); attribute-derived scores remain predictive when attributes and response positions are fitted jointly, and a position-only predictor is also informative (\S{}6). (3) \textbf{Beneficial personalisation needs a competent population baseline.} Own-versus-other contrasts do not measure benefit; gains over the population model are small (+0.3 to +0.7 choice points where established), not established on MultiPref, and the measured gain changes by about 0.6 points with the population-training procedure alone (\S{}6). Semi-synthetic positive controls characterise the evaluated learners' recovery of specified injected preference families (\S{}5.6); a screened attribute learner and the covariance-calibrated procedure are reported as supporting tools (Appendix G, F). Protocols, amendments and failed hypotheses are in Appendices A, B, E and F.

\section{Related work}

\textbf{Pluralistic and distributional reward models.} Distributional preference learning (\citealp{siththaranjan2024dpl}), diverging-preference detection (\citealp{zhang2025diverging}), PRISM (\citealp{kirk2024prism}), MultiPref (\citealp{miranda2025hybrid}) and DICES (\citealp{aroyo2023dices}) motivate modelling disagreement; jury learning (\citealp{gordon2022jury}), multi-annotator models (\citealp{davani2022dealing}), disagreement deconvolution (\citealp{gordon2021deconvolution}), per-rater toxicity models (\citealp{kumar2021toxic}), opinion-based prediction of unseen annotators (\citealp{fleisig2023majority}) and multilevel models of DICES raters (\citealp{homan2024intersectionality}) predict individual annotators. Preference strength enters reward training as a margin (\citealp{touvron2023llama2}; \citealp{wang2025helpsteer2pref}); ties have classical paired-comparison models (\citealp{rao1967ties}; \citealp{davidson1970ties}). \textbf{Personalised reward models} infer a user latent from a few examples (\citealp{poddar2024vpl}; \citealp{chen2025pal}), use a low-rank reward subspace (\citealp{bose2025lore}), persona prompts induced from history (\citealp{ryan2025synthesizeme}), per-user embeddings (\citealp{li2024prlhf}), combinations of base rewards (\citealp{shenfeld2025pref}; \citealp{luo2025pca}) or neural processes (\citealp{kobalczyk2024fewshot}); others personalise the policy (\citealp{jang2023soups}; \citealp{feng2024modular}), adapt to groups (\citealp{zhao2024gpo}; \citealp{chakraborty2024maxmin}), study personalisation versus aggregation (\citealp{park2024heterogeneous}) or condition a decoder on value profiles of held-out raters (\citealp{sorensen2025valueprofiles}). Benchmarks often simulate users (\citealp{castricato2025persona}; \citealp{zollo2025personalllm}); real multi-rater data with diverse candidates are emerging (\citealp{zhang2026community}). VPL's latent can collapse under sparse data (\citealp{kim2026swap}), and methods differ widely in a multi-faceted comparison (\citealp{dong2025personalization}). Rater groups use safety severity scales differently (\citealp{mishra2026decoding}); demographics are often weak predictors (\citealp{orlikowski2023ecological}). \textbf{Measurement theory and annotator models.} Rater severity and scale use are standard facets of many-facet Rasch measurement (\citealp{linacre1989mfrm}; \citealp{eckes2011mfrm}) and response styles a classical survey topic (\citealp{baumgartner2001response}); user and item biases carry much of the gain in collaborative filtering (\citealp{koren2009mf}), user-specific ordinal thresholds predict personal rating distributions (\citealp{koren2011ordrec}), and annotator models estimate rater-specific labelling behaviour (\citealp{dawid1979em}; \citealp{raykar2010crowds}; \citealp{hovy2013mace}; \citealp{deng2023annotate}; \citealp{plank2022problem}; \citealp{uma2021learning}). Our offsets are of this kind and are not new; what is new is testing personalised reward models against them on new raters and asking what these models could detect.

\section{Data, and where the predictable structure sits}

\textbf{Data.} MultiPref (\citealp{miranda2025hybrid}; 10,461 comparisons; 4 ratings each by 2 crowd and 2 expert raters; 227 persistent raters; 5 ordered levels A-clearly \ldots{} B-clearly). PRISM (\citealp{kirk2024prism}; 68,371 responses each rated 1--100 by one of 1,396 users; binned to 5 fixed levels). DICES-350 (\citealp{aroyo2023dices}; 350 conversations fully crossed with 104 quality-checked raters; safe / unsure / unsafe).

\textbf{A learner-free measurement (Appendix D).} Before fitting any personalised model we measured, on MultiPref, where held-out predictable structure sits: beyond item location, rater-specific thresholds improve held-out likelihood about 25 times more than item-specific dispersion (+0.036 vs +0.0014 nats per rating), the item-level shape of the rating distribution that replicates across disjoint rater panels is small (split-half reliability 0.25), and about a tenth of it is decodable from text. Within this decomposition, the predictable structure beyond item location is therefore mostly rater-side scale use, while the item-level shape that replicates across disjoint panels is small and only partly in the text. These two facts come from different estimands and do not say that disagreement is ``not in the text''. The stage-1 decision rule we had written down returned \textbf{``mixed''}, because G/X was not $\leq$ 10 \% (interval 5.7--11.9 \%); the factorised model of \S{}4 was chosen with that outcome known.

\section{A three-parameter scale-use baseline}
\begin{figure*}[t]\centering\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{fig_styles.pdf}
\caption{What three numbers per rater change. Left: the marginal distribution of MultiPref ratings. Right: the same distribution passed through the fitted scale-use offsets (extreme, slight, tie) of three real new raters (k = 50) closest to the 90th / 10th percentile of intensity (extreme minus slight) among raters whose tie offset is at or below the median, and closest to the 90th percentile of the tie offset. The offsets are text-independent: they mainly reshape predicted intensity and ties; changes in the preferred side are small.}\label{fig:styles}\end{figure*}

Let $\ell$(x) $\in$ \(\mathbb{R}^K\) be the class logits of a text-only distributional reward model for item x (K ordered rating levels). For rater r we add a text-independent offset vector (Figure 1): \(p(y \mid x, r) = \mathrm{softmax}(\ell(x) + b_r)\). Variants: \textbf{DF} (\(b_r \in \mathbb{R}^K\), free), \textbf{DFs} (two-sided preference scales only: \(b_r\) tied symmetrically, (e, s, t, s, e) --- extreme, slight, tie --- 3 parameters, of which 2 are identifiable because the softmax is invariant to a common shift; no A-vs-B position term). Rater offsets have a Gaussian prior centred on the training-rater mean; a new rater's offsets are the MAP estimate from k of their ratings with the text model frozen. Baselines under the same protocol: \textbf{D} (text-only distributional RM), \textbf{DG} (group offsets: crowd/expert, race, region), \textbf{DL} (low-rank text-interacting latent: $\ell$(x) + \((Wx + w_0)\cdot z_r\), d = 8, the personalised-RM family). An interpretable probit family (rater shift / log-scale on an ordered-probit text head) is reported as a secondary analysis.

\textbf{Training and adaptation.} The text model and the training raters' offsets are fitted jointly by penalised maximum likelihood, \[\min \sum_{(i,r,y)} -\log p(y \mid x_i, r) + \lambda_{\text{text}} \|V\|^2 + \lambda_r \sum_r \|b_r\|^2,\] with \(\lambda_{\text{text}}\) chosen on the text-only model and \(\lambda_r\) on held-out items of the training raters. A new rater \(r^*\) is adapted from \(k\) ratings \(\{(x_j, y_j)\}\) by \(\hat b_{r^*} = \arg\min_b \sum_j -\log \mathrm{softmax}(\ell(x_j) + b)_{y_j} + \lambda_r \|b - \bar b\|^2\), with \(\bar b\) the training-rater mean (the prior mean; \(\hat b = \bar b\) at \(k = 0\)) and the text model frozen --- a convex problem in 3--5 parameters, solved in milliseconds. Any text model that outputs graded log-probabilities can be used: we use a ridge head on frozen LLM features (\S{}5.1--5.2), a LoRA-fine-tuned LLM reward model (\S{}5.3) and the decoder of a published personalised reward model (\S{}5.4).

\textbf{Protocol.} 5 paired splits; split f holds out rater fold f (20 \% of raters, stratified by group) and prompt-group fold f (20 \% of prompt groups); models are trained on training raters $\times$ training items with penalties chosen on a validation split; each test rater is adapted on k $\in$ \{0, 5, 10, 20, 50\} of their ratings of \emph{training} items (5 random draws) and evaluated on their ratings of \emph{held-out} items. Primary metric: held-out log-likelihood per rating; also RPS and preference-direction accuracy; rater-cluster bootstrap (B = 2,000). Because test raters share test items on MultiPref and DICES, the headline k = 20 contrasts were also re-estimated with a two-way bootstrap that resamples raters and prompt groups independently: on graded labels every log-likelihood interval widens by at most 0.007 on either side and no log-likelihood conclusion changes (original rows); direction-accuracy differences of about a point or less (the style model's +0.5 on MultiPref, VPL's direction advantage over the style model) lose significance and are marked where they appear. Text model in \S{}5.1--5.2: frozen Qwen2.5-7B features with a ridge head; \S{}5.3: a LoRA-fine-tuned reward model.

\subsection{On a fine-tuned reward model}

With a LoRA-fine-tuned Qwen2.5-7B reward model as the text model, the three offsets still add +0.100 {[}+0.071, +0.130{]} nats per rating at k = 20 --- as on the frozen-feature head --- and the gains of fine-tuning and of personalisation add. Against in-context personalisation (a LoRA model given the rater's k ratings in the prompt, three seeds per fold) the offsets are better by +0.041 {[}+0.027, +0.054{]} nats and +1.9 {[}+0.9, +2.7{]} direction points (Appendix B).

\subsection{Published personalised reward models}

We reimplemented VPL (\citealp{poddar2024vpl}) and PAL-B (\citealp{chen2025pal}) from their official code as heads on the same frozen per-response embeddings and evaluated them under the same new-rater protocol, refitting the text-only and style models on those embeddings. On graded labels, at k = 20 the style model is better than VPL by +0.092 {[}+0.068, +0.119{]} nats per rating ({[}+0.063, +0.124{]} when prompt groups are also resampled) and than PAL-B by +0.158. VPL's latent does carry rater information once its posterior collapse is prevented (KL weight selected as 0 or 0.001; +0.087 over its own unpersonalised version), but only lifts it to the level of the text-only model; PAL-B's few-shot user weights give no gain. Their user representations are unrelated to response style (cross-validated R$^2$ $\approx$ 0 against the style offsets). On binary labels --- the setting in which both methods were proposed --- every method's personalisation gain is at most 0.002 nats (small but nonzero for PAL-B with K = 4, and for VPL and the style model at k = 50), and VPL's text head is the best binary model (the style model is 0.010 nats behind it and 0.005 behind the text-only model, differences of the size of item-sampling noise): on these panels the largest demonstrated gains are captured by calibration of graded levels and ties, which binary preference data discard. VPL's per-response reward head is slightly better at predicting \emph{which} response is preferred (0.677 vs 0.668), an effect present without personalisation and not significant once prompt groups are also resampled (style model $-$ VPL: $-$0.9 points {[}$-$2.7, +0.8{]}). The offsets are also a plug-in: added to VPL (fitted on the same k ratings, VPL frozen) they improve it by +0.088 {[}+0.066, +0.111{]} nats per rating at k = 20, come within $-$0.004 {[}$-$0.014, +0.005{]} of the style model on likelihood (non-inferiority not established) and keep VPL's direction accuracy (+1.1 points over the style model alone; rater bootstrap only).

\subsection{Could these methods have detected content personalisation? A semi-synthetic positive control}

A ranking null is informative only if the methods would detect content-dependent preferences where they exist. We keep MultiPref's raters, items, folds and k-shot draws and replace the labels by semi-synthetic ones: a generator (the text-only head plus scale-use offsets) gives each rating a distribution, to which a rater-specific content term \((\theta_r^\top g_i)\,(-2,-1,0,1,2)\) is added; \(g_i\) are two response-pair features (log length ratio and a random embedding direction orthogonal to consensus quality), \(\theta_r \sim N(0, \tau^2 I)\), and \(\tau\) is calibrated so that an oracle knowing \(\theta_r\) gains +1.7, +5.2, +10.1 or +21.1 direction points (\(\tau = 0\): null control). The contrast is \textbf{own adapted parameters minus another test rater's} (a derangement within fold, k and draw). We fixed beforehand that the protocol has power at a strength if some content-interacting method has a lower 95 \% bound above +1 point at k = 20; the +10 and +21 panels are exploratory.

A later real-data control (Jester; Appendix B) exposed a mis-scaled adaptation prior in our latent models; corrected post hoc, in this injection family and draw the ordinal latent passes the criterion at +10 points (+2.82 {[}+2.02, +3.63{]}; the softmax latent, +0.62 {[}+0.17, +1.06{]}, does not) but not at +5, its gain over no adaptation is not significant (+0.84 {[}$-$0.12, +1.80{]}), and neither latent shows an established direction gain on real MultiPref. A reference learner given the two injected features recovers +3.1 and +8.0 points of the +5.2 and +10.1-point oracle gains, so the gap implicates representation and inductive bias (Appendix B). These results characterise recovery for specified injected families on panels noisier than the real data; they are not formal exclusion bounds.

\subsection{How much room is there? Conflicts among aspect judgements are rare}

With the rater's own helpfulness, truthfulness and harmlessness labels as inputs, a pooled trade-off gets 97.5 \% of MultiPref directions right and a rater-specific one fitted on all training ratings adds only +0.10 {[}$-$0.24, +0.43{]} points (+0.03 at k = 20): the three aspect judgements conflict in only 4.0 \% of held-out non-tie comparisons (details in Appendix B).

\subsection{A learner that finds the axes}

A screened empirical-Bayes learner over 24 interpretable attribute differences (SAP) recovers the semi-synthetic content preferences that the published learners miss, with near-zero effects on null panels, and abstains on real MultiPref under a prospectively specified rule (Appendix G).

\subsection{Developers rating code readability}

On two code-readability studies with persistent developer identities (Buse--Weimer, 121 raters, \citealp{buse2010readability}; Dorn, 3,393 developers, \citealp{dorn2012readability}) and Qwen2.5-Coder-7B features, scale use transfers to new developers (offsets +0.054 {[}+0.036, +0.074{]} nats per rating at k = 20 and +0.040 {[}+0.031, +0.049{]} at k = 10), while content adaptation over 22 readability attributes is inconclusive at the pre-set budgets (Appendices A--B).

\section{What personalisation buys: graded-rating prediction, user signal, and small choice gains}

\textbf{Binarisation removes the symmetric scale-use term; evaluated adaptation gains on MultiPref are small.} On MultiPref with ties dropped and labels binarised, every evaluated method's personalisation gain is at most 0.002 nats per rating (\S{}5.4), and the scale-use baseline's binary accuracy is essentially unchanged from the text-only model's. On these graded panels the largest demonstrated gains concern graded levels and ties --- which binary preference data, the format in which most personalised reward models are trained and evaluated, discard (Community Alignment shows that binary choices can also carry predictable user differences, \S{}6). \textbf{Ranking moves little.} Symmetric offsets mainly change how strongly a rater is predicted to prefer a response: on graded MultiPref the scale-use baseline raises direction accuracy by only +0.005 {[}+0.001, +0.008{]} at k = 20 (about half of it already at k = 0), while RPS drops from 0.1593 to 0.1532. On single-response safety ratings the same offsets do move the safe/unsafe decision (+4.8 points on DICES), because there a rater's threshold \emph{is} the decision. Text-only direction accuracy on MultiPref (0.674) is already as high as the agreement in direction between two human raters of the same item (0.673 {[}0.666, 0.680{]}): a population model predicts a new rater about as well as another person does. \textbf{Seen raters.} With all their training ratings (median 134), training raters' own parameters beat another rater's by $-$0.11 {[}$-$0.57, +0.36{]} direction points for the low-rank latent (original prior; exploratory, Appendix B). Against its own k = 0 version, no method's direction accuracy on MultiPref rises by more than 0.2 points at k = 20, and every upper 95 \% bound stays below +0.8 points even when prompt groups are resampled together with raters (Appendix B). \textbf{The ranking null replicates on real users' own choices.} PRISM users each saw 2--4 responses to their own prompt, scored them and chose one; pairing the chosen response with every other one gives 41,199 native pairwise choices of 1,396 users. \emph{Identity on identical items (Stage 13, frozen).} CA contains 2,928 exact prompt--four-response items shown to at least two users, covering 49,616 first-turn conversations (median 15 users per item, one language per item, fixed response order). For two users of the same item who chose different responses a and b, the population utilities cancel and the adapted models predict the assignment from \(S = (\delta_u - \delta_v)^\top (x_a - x_b)\), with model-implied probability \(\sigma(S)\). With validated isotropic adaptation over length, paragraphs and list/heading lines (k = 20 support events on other prompts; the Stage-12 procedure unchanged), the \textbf{conditional assignment log score gains +0.066 {[}+0.034, +0.091{]} nats over chance and assignment accuracy is 62.2 \% {[}58.6, 65.6{]}} (4,608 disagreeing pairs from 1,336 users on 1,116 items; item $\times$ user bootstrap). The estimand is narrow: given two disagreeing users, their support choices and the two responses they picked, assign the responses; it conditions on the fitted deviations and population model.

\emph{Position.} The four fixed positions differ systematically in these attributes (label-blind check), so we refitted with position indicators added (after the result). The attribute-derived part of the jointly fitted scores keeps +0.060 {[}+0.032, +0.084{]} nats, a position-only predictor is also informative (63.8 \%), and the joint model's paired gain over position-only is +0.026 {[}$-$0.0002, +0.046{]}, so additional assignment value from attributes beyond position is unresolved. With correlated predictors under one prior this does not separate content from position: we claim user-specific choice predictability on identical alternatives, not a content preference, and not a psychological position habit.

\emph{Benefit over the population model.} On all test events of the shared items in the test folds --- a broader panel than the 1,116 items that contribute disagreeing pairs to the assignment test (2,383 items, 1,436 users; population accuracy 72.4 \%) --- isotropic adaptation gains \textbf{+0.70 {[}+0.24, +1.14{]} pairwise accuracy points and +0.018 {[}+0.014, +0.023{]} nats} --- the pre-set user-bootstrap rule for benefit is met, but the user $\times$ item interval for accuracy covers 0 ({[}$-$0.07, +1.43{]}) while log-likelihood improves under both, so an accuracy benefit that generalises across items is unresolved; nor is the gain shown to be below one point. On MultiPref the frozen isotropic procedure did not establish improvement ($-$0.28 {[}$-$0.64, +0.08{]} points, 202 held-out annotators). On CA the estimated covariance shows no advantage over isotropic shrinkage (turn 1 $-$0.17 {[}$-$0.29, $-$0.05{]}, turn 2 +0.07 {[}$-$0.07, +0.22{]} points); on MultiPref it beats isotropic under the rater bootstrap (+0.23 {[}+0.04, +0.42{]}) without establishing improvement over the population model (two-way includes 0; Appendix F). Validated scales imply strong shrinkage, and realised gains are far below model-implied ones (the c = 1 covariance-prior learner: +1.9 points implied under a Gaussian event-level simulation, +0.13 realised; Appendix F).

\emph{Transport to later conversations (Stage 13, frozen).} We froze each user's first-turn deviation and added it, without rescaling, to a third-turn population model that sees the conversation history (the earlier prompts and the user's preferred responses). We scored it on the third turns of conversations not used for support. Of 1,901 users with a frozen first-turn deviation, 1,808 have at least five third-turn events with a complete history and 953 have at least five outside their support conversations. The result is accuracy-positive under user clustering and likelihood-inconclusive: accuracy rises by +0.43 {[}+0.13, +0.74{]} points (user $\times$ prompt interval {[}$-$0.08, +0.92{]}), log-likelihood by +0.0015 {[}$-$0.0006, +0.0035{]} nats. The pre-set rule requires both lower bounds above 0, so additional predictive value of unrescaled first-turn coefficients beyond a history-conditioned population model is \textbf{not supported for this procedure and panel}. This neither shows that transport has no effect nor that preferences are unstable, and it does not test other ways of carrying information across turns.

\emph{The measured gain depends on the population baseline.} On 1,799 common users, one-dimensional length adaptation gains +0.88 / +1.08 points (random / prompt-separated test events) against a population model fitted on low-volume annotators, as in the WIMHF protocol (\citealp{movva2025wimhf}), and +0.26 / +0.47 against one fitted on all other-fold users: the population-procedure effect is +0.62 {[}+0.41, +0.81{]} and +0.61 {[}+0.30, +0.93{]} (paired), while the prompt-split contrast is inconclusive (+0.20 {[}$-$0.20, +0.59{]}). The two procedures differ in volume, composition and prior estimation, so no mechanism is isolated. (Our line-count proxy is not WIMHF's discovered feature; with it, list/heading adaptation does not beat the population coefficient --- a related protocol study, not a failed reproduction.)

\emph{Earlier evidence.} Before this test, own adapted parameters beat another user's for four of six methods (LoRe +3.27 {[}+2.85, +3.70{]}); own $-$ other does not measure benefit and task mix could create it (Appendix F), so the identical-item test supersedes it.

\section{Recommendations}

\begin{enumerate}
\def\labelenumi{(\roman{enumi})}
\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{Separate three outcomes.} Report graded-rating likelihood (calibration and resolution), a test of user-specific choice predictability, and choice or ranking gains over the population model as different results; a gain on one does not imply the others. (ii) \textbf{Test user differences on identical items} where the data allow it (an assignment test between disagreeing users, as in \S{}6): own-versus-other contrasts on different prompts confound user differences with task mix and do not measure benefit. (iii) \textbf{Report a competent population baseline and its training procedure.} The measured personalisation gain changed by about 0.6 points with the population-training procedure (\S{}6). (iv) \textbf{Report a scale-use baseline} (a few text-independent offsets per rater) next to any personalised reward model on graded labels, and evaluate content adaptation with those offsets retained. (v) \textbf{Evaluate on unseen raters and held-out prompts}, with permutation and positive controls. (vi) Keep graded labels and ties when individual differences matter.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Discussion}

\textbf{What do personalisation gains measure?} Three things often reported as one come apart. Graded-rating gains for new raters are largely scale use: offsets capture them, probes find near-zero R$^2$ for them in the evaluated user representations, and adding offsets to VPL recovers most of the point-estimate gain (+0.088; non-inferiority not established). User-specific choice predictability is real on identical CA items (62 \%), though not separated from position. Choice gains over a competent population model are small and fragile (Table 5).

\textbf{Why did published models learn little about new raters here?} Published evaluations differ in users (synthetic or seen), label formats, representations and population baselines; the last alone changes the measured gain by about 0.6 points on CA. Unresolved reproduction discrepancies prevent attributing the rest to evaluation design; method conclusions concern the evaluated implementations.

\section{Limitations and analysis log}

The main tables use frozen-feature text heads (a LoRA reward model only in \S{}5.3), and compressed features weaken PAL-B as a detector (\S{}5.6). VPL, PAL and LoRe are our reimplementations as heads on frozen embeddings; published numbers were not reproduced (LoRe's PRISM gain not even with its own embeddings). The injection controls cover MultiPref only (corrected models: one draw; Jester recovery on seen jokes only), and the latent correction is post hoc. Aspect analyses use MultiPref's concurrent labels (SAP: Appendix G). The first three datasets are English, and CA's phrase-based attributes cover English only; per-rater calibration is not shown to change a downstream decision. The CA analyses reuse earlier-examined data (Appendix A); the identical-item test conditions on fitted models; features omit conversation history except in the third-turn test; and identity is not separated from response position.

\section*{Impact Statement}

This paper evaluates personalised reward models, which are meant to let language models serve people whose preferences differ. It finds that reported personalisation gains can reflect rating-scale use, user-associated choice predictability or real improvements over a population model, and that these need separate tests: gains on graded ratings were largely scale use, users' choices on identical alternatives were predictable from their other choices, and improvements over a population model were small and dataset-dependent. Predicting individuals' choices also raises privacy and manipulation concerns that a deployment would need to address; we report no individual-level results. The datasets are public, were collected with consent by their creators, and are used under their licences; rater identifiers are used only as anonymous indices.

\textbf{Scale use and the shared reward model.} Training the text model jointly with rater offsets and then using the population-mean offsets (k = 0) does not give a better consensus model than ordinary training: $-$0.003 {[}$-$0.010, +0.003{]} nats on MultiPref (direction +0.3 points, n.s.), $-$0.022 {[}$-$0.037, $-$0.008{]} on DICES, +0.001 on PRISM, $-$0.009 {[}$-$0.016, $-$0.004{]} with the LoRA reward model. Ordinary training averages scale use out; it matters for predicting a person, not for learning the content preference.

\textbf{What the offsets encode.} In the probit family, rater \emph{scale} matters more than rater \emph{shift} on MultiPref (+0.036 {[}+0.020, +0.057{]} at k = 20), but rater \emph{shift} dominates on DICES ($-$0.053) and PRISM ($-$0.031): leniency/strictness on single-response rating scales, intensity on two-sided preference scales.

\textbf{What this does and does not say about pluralism.} Our results do not show that people agree about content: the item-level disagreement that replicates across rater panels is real (split-half reliability 0.25 on MultiPref) and about a tenth of it is decodable from text. They show that, for predicting an individual new rater from few ratings, that content-level disagreement is a small part of what is predictable, and that demographic or crowd/expert groups add nothing beyond the text.

\textbf{Fine-tuned and in-context reward models (\S{}5.3, full text).} With a LoRA-fine-tuned Qwen2.5-7B reward model (D-LoRA, trained per fold on the training raters' rating distributions) as the text model, the three-number style model still adds +0.100 {[}+0.071, +0.130{]} nats per rating at k = 20 --- the same as its gain on the frozen-feature head (+0.100). Fine-tuning improves the text-only model by +0.027 {[}+0.016, +0.039{]} (direction accuracy +1.4 points) and the style model by the same +0.027: the two gains add. Personalising the frozen-feature model with 20 ratings per rater gains more than fine-tuning the text model (+0.072 {[}+0.041, +0.106{]}). DFs keeps the direction accuracy of D-LoRA (+0.002 {[}$-$0.002, +0.006{]}). Against in-context personalisation --- a separate LoRA model on the same base, trained with ten of the rater's other ratings in the prompt and evaluated with the new rater's k ratings in the prompt --- the three offsets on D-LoRA are better at k = 20. The in-context model's result varies with its training seed (two of fifteen runs failed, gaining $-$0.078 and $-$0.055 over D-LoRA against +0.021 to +0.125 for the others; the two failed runs have the highest training loss), so it was trained with three seeds per fold, and we fixed in advance that ``better'' requires a lower bound above zero for every seed and for the seed average. Averaged over seeds --- the expected result of one training run --- the offsets are better by +0.041 {[}+0.027, +0.054{]} nats per rating (209 raters; per seed +0.062, +0.018 and +0.042, every lower bound at least +0.005) and more accurate on which response the rater prefers (+1.9 points {[}+0.9, +2.7{]}). The in-context model does learn from the history (+0.051 {[}+0.024, +0.079{]} over D-LoRA on average), at several thousand prompt tokens per prediction, but its direction accuracy is below D-LoRA's ($-$1.7 points {[}$-$2.6, $-$0.8{]} on average; $-$0.6 to $-$3.0 per seed).

\textbf{Value conflicts (\S{}5.7, full text).} The positive control asks what the methods could detect; MultiPref also lets us ask how much there is to detect. Every MultiPref annotation carries the annotator's own helpfulness, truthfulness and harmlessness preferences next to the overall one, so a rater's way of trading these values off is observable. We fit an ordinal model of the overall preference from the annotator's own aspect labels (their values and absolute values) with rater-specific cut-points (scale use) and either a pooled trade-off or a rater-specific trade-off, adapted on k of the rater's training-item annotations and evaluated on held-out items \textbf{with the rater's own aspect labels as inputs} --- an oracle for the content side. The pooled trade-off weights harmlessness most (6.1, five-fold mean), then helpfulness (4.0) and truthfulness (2.0). With the rater's own aspect labels as inputs the pooled model already gets 97.5 \% of directions right (74 \% on conflict items): aspect and overall labels are produced together by the same annotator, so this ceiling concerns a small residual and is a property of MultiPref's concurrent labelling.

Linear probes show the injected direction is available to VPL and PAL-B (out-of-fold R$^2$ 0.65 compressed, 1.00 full) but not to the low-rank latent's joint embedding (0.02); re-injected inside that embedding, the latent detects +10 points (3 of 4 draws) but not +5 (0 of 4); these probes predate the corrected adaptation prior, with which the ordinal latent detects the original +10-point panel (Stage 14). With semantic axes (formatting, hedging/refusal) no method detects even +9.7 points. Twenty ratings permit useful recovery in these injected families when the generating features are supplied; the observed gap implicates feature representation and the learners' inductive biases. These are comparisons with synthetic panels of specific injected forms (noisier than the real data), not formal exclusion bounds; details and seed variability in Appendix B. Generator and calibration as first described: We keep MultiPref's structure (raters, items, who rated what, folds and k-shot draws) and replace the labels by semi-synthetic ones. A generator --- the text-only head plus scale-use offsets, fitted on all ratings --- gives each rating a distribution, to which a rater-specific content term \((\theta_r^\top g_i)\,(-2,-1,0,1,2)\) is added to the logits; \(g_i\) are two features of the response pair (the log length ratio and a random direction in the per-response embedding space, orthogonal to the consensus quality direction) and \(\theta_r \sim N(0, \tau^2 I)\). The scale \(\tau\) is calibrated on the generator alone so that an oracle that knows \(\theta_r\) gains +1.7, +5.2, +10.1 or +21.1 points of direction accuracy on the test ratings; \(\tau = 0\) is a null control. Every method is then run under the paper's protocol. Because a method whose unpersonalised state is not the population state can improve with anyone's ratings, the contrast is \textbf{the method adapted on the rater's own k ratings minus the same method given another test rater's adapted parameters} (a random derangement within fold, k and draw). Before the runs we fixed that the protocol has power at a strength if some content-interacting method has a lower 95 \% bound above +1 point at k = 20; the +10 and +21 panels and the untuned-prior low-rank runs were added after the first results and are exploratory.

\textbf{5.7 How much room is there? (full text moved from \S{}5).} Every MultiPref annotation carries the annotator's own helpfulness, truthfulness and harmlessness preferences, so a rater's trade-off between these values is observable. With the rater's own aspect labels as inputs (an oracle for the content side), a pooled trade-off already gets 97.5 \% of directions right, and a rater-specific trade-off fitted on all of the rater's training ratings adds only +0.10 {[}$-$0.24, +0.43{]} points (+0.44 even in-sample). The three recorded aspect judgements point in opposite directions in only 4.0 \% of held-out non-tie comparisons; there individual trade-offs matter (in-sample +9.7 points), but they are too rare to move accuracy. A calibrated test with the item's consensus aspects from other annotators finds individual weights transferring worse than the population's ($-$0.28 {[}$-$0.56, $-$0.06{]} at k = 20; exploratory, Appendix B).

\textbf{5.9 Developers rating code readability (full text moved from \S{}5).} Two code-readability studies have persistent developer identities: Buse--Weimer (121 raters $\times$ 100 Java snippets) and Dorn (3,393 developers with at least ten ratings of 360 Java/Python/CUDA snippets). With Qwen2.5-Coder-7B features, scale use transfers to new developers: offsets gain +0.054 {[}+0.036, +0.074{]} nats per rating at k = 20 (Buse--Weimer) and +0.040 {[}+0.031, +0.049{]} at k = 10 (Dorn). SAP with 22 classical readability attributes (line and identifier length, indentation, nesting, comments, operators, \ldots) gives +0.40 {[}$-$0.25, +1.06{]} points at the pre-set k = 20 on Buse--Weimer (+1.30 {[}+0.46, +2.18{]} at k = 50, exploratory) and $-$0.19 {[}$-$0.81, +0.42{]} on Dorn at its pre-set k = 10; the low-rank latent is inconclusive on Buse--Weimer and switched off by validation on Dorn. \section{Implementation}

\textbf{Rater identities.} All four panels carry persistent rater identifiers (MultiPref pseudonyms, 227 evaluators, median 166 ratings, never twice on one item; DICES rater IDs, 123 raters $\times$ 350 items in the release, 104 after its quality filter (used throughout); PRISM user IDs, one survey record each; Community Alignment annotator IDs). Demographics never vary within an ID on DICES and in fewer than 0.3 \% of IDs on Community Alignment. On MultiPref, which has no demographics, an evaluator's tie rate and extreme-rating rate in the first and second half of their ratings (by timestamp) correlate at 0.82 and 0.78 across evaluators, against $-$0.03 and 0.00 (largest 0.17) with evaluator labels shuffled (\texttt{check\_rater\_ids.py}). Shared accounts cannot be ruled out.

Text features: last-token final-layer states of Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct under a 5-point judge prompt (MultiPref, PRISM) or a 3-level safety prompt (DICES), label-free PCA to 256 dimensions. Folds: 5 rater folds (stratified by group) paired with 5 prompt-group folds (split f holds out rater fold f and prompt-group fold f; the other crossings are not used); validation: 15 \% of training prompt groups. k-shot draws: 5 per k, identical across all models (RC-LoRA: draw 0 only). PRISM item folds are by conversation. Bootstrap: rater clusters, B = 2,000. LoRA: r = 16 on all attention and MLP projections, lr \(10^{-4}\), one epoch, accumulation 8. VPL / PAL-B: official architectures (latent 512, hidden 512; \(d_{\text{pref}}\) 512, mlp2-gelu projectors, angle learner) with an ordered-logistic link for graded labels; VPL's KL weight selected from \{0.01 (official), 0.001, 0\} and PAL-B's adaptation ridge by k-shot validation (non-official choices made to prevent collapse and over-fitting).

\section{Where is the predictable structure? Estimators and results}

\textbf{Estimators.} For an item with ratings on \(K\) ordered levels, let \(h\) be the empirical rating distribution and \(C\) the cumulative operator. Split the item's raters at random into disjoint panels \(P_1\) (\(a\) raters) and \(P_2\), and let \(q\) be a cross-fitted \emph{location-only} forecast with exactly the mode and mean of \(P_1\) (built from other items in the same mode-and-mean cell). With \(r_1 = C(h_1 - q)\), \(r_2 = C(h_2 - q)\), \(M = \|r_1\|^2/n\) and \(X = \langle r_1, r_2\rangle/n\), the forecasting value of the raw distribution over its own location obeys the identity \(S(h_1) - S(q) = M - 2X\) under the ranked probability score, so \(\rho = X/M\) is a split-half reliability of the distribution's \emph{shape} and the raw distribution beats its location exactly when \(\rho > 1/2\). Text decodability is the proper-score gain \(G = (2\langle \hat r, r_2\rangle - \|\hat r\|^2)/n\) of a residual \(\hat r\) decoded from frozen text features, cross-fitted over prompt groups, reported as the fraction \(G/X\) of the reproducible shape. The rater--item decomposition fits ordered-probit models \(Y^*_{ir} = \theta_i + \delta_r + \sigma_i\sigma_r\varepsilon\) with item locations \(\theta_i\), rater shifts \(\delta_r\), rater scales \(\sigma_r\) and item dispersions \(\sigma_i\), compares held-out likelihood over random cells (every held-out rater and item appears in training), and chooses all penalties by inner cross-validation.

\textbf{Reproducibility.} With the split-half estimator defined above, the reliability of the rating distribution's shape beyond its exact mode and mean is $\rho$ = 0.25 {[}0.22, 0.29{]} on MultiPref overall preference with three raters (helpful 0.29, harmless 0.21): the raw few-rater distribution forecasts a new rater worse than its own location ($\rho$ \textless{} $\tfrac12$). \textbf{Text decodability.} A cross-fitted ridge decoder on frozen Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct features, scored on an independent rater panel, recovers G/X = 10.5 \% {[}5.7 \%, 11.9 \%{]} of the reproducible shape (within-cell permutation control $\leq$ 0; positive control 0.35) --- comparable to the upper end for ordinal quality ratings (upper bounds 0.4--10 \%) and below NLI (17--26 \%). \textbf{Rater vs item.} A long-format ordered-probit decomposition with real rater identities and penalties tuned by inner cross-validation: rater-specific thresholds improve held-out likelihood by +0.036 nats per rating over item location alone, item-specific dispersion by a further +0.0014 (ratio 25; 19--36 across the five folds); of the rater part, rater scale contributes +0.031 and rater shift +0.005.

\section{Protocols and what did not hold}

All analyses are documented in an analysis log: timestamped internal protocols written before each stage (not public registrations, so they document rather than certify the order of decisions); changes after results are logged as amendments with reasons, and the symmetric style model DFs and its hypothesis were added after the first full result. Not supported or not robust: (i) the stage-1 decision rule returned ``mixed'' (text decodability not $\leq$ 10 \%); (ii) non-inferiority of the 5-parameter DF to the low-rank latent (DF $-$ DL $\geq$ $-$0.005) holds with the training-rater prior but not with a few-shot-tuned prior on MultiPref ($-$0.015; $-$0.020 with the corrected prior of Stage 14) and is formally not met on PRISM (bound $-$0.006; with the corrected prior the latent is 0.004 nats ahead, bound $-$0.007); (iii) ``scale beats shift'' holds only on MultiPref; (iv) the originally registered probit-family hypothesis is not supported (F1 $-$ D = +0.020 {[}$-$0.001, +0.041{]}, the primary probit contrast after Amendment 1; homoscedastic F $-$ D = $-$0.029 {[}$-$0.049, $-$0.008{]}; superseded when the primary family was moved to the distributional head); (v) the prediction that published user latents encode style failed (R$^2$ $\approx$ 0); (vi) the hybrid's non-inferiority to DFs is formally not met (bound $-$0.014). Five baseline problems found during the study were fixed in favour of the baselines --- a missing constant column in the low-rank latent, a scale non-identifiability in the probit group model, VPL's posterior collapse, PAL-B's near-unregularised adaptation, and the latent models' mis-scaled adaptation prior (Stage 14) --- and the runs before each fix are reported.
