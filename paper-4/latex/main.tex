\begin{abstract}
Personalised reward models promise to represent how individual people judge model outputs. We test what they learn about raters they have never seen, under one protocol on three multi-rater datasets (MultiPref, DICES, PRISM): k of a new rater's ratings for adaptation, evaluation on held-out items. A text-independent scale-use baseline from measurement theory --- a few offsets per rater on the rating levels (on two-sided preferences, tied offsets for ``clearly'', ``slightly'' and ``tie'', two of them identifiable) --- improves held-out log-likelihood by 0.08--0.10 nats per rating at k = 20 on all three datasets. A demographic or crowd/expert grouping adds nothing; a tuned low-rank user latent ties it; reimplemented VPL and PAL and an in-context LoRA reward model gain less; on binary labels every gain is at most 0.002 nats. No method improves the prediction of which response a new rater prefers: on MultiPref and on 41,199 native pairwise choices of PRISM users, every method's gain in direction or choice accuracy over its unpersonalised version has an upper 95 \% bound below one point. A semi-synthetic positive control shows what this null means. With injected content preferences that an oracle could exploit for +5 or +10 points of direction accuracy, the same methods on compressed (PCA-256) features recover less than half a point at k = 20 (point estimates); only at +21 points do VPL and PAL recover +6.9 and +4.6. On full features PAL detects +10 points at k = 20, yet on the real data it recovers nothing (own versus another rater's ratings: +0.11 and +0.19 points). A well-specified reference learner that knows which features carry the preference recovers about 60 \% of a +5-point preference from the same 20 ratings, so the limit is finding the content dimension, not the number of ratings. Given the dimensions MultiPref annotators rated --- helpfulness, truthfulness, harmlessness --- the same reference learner finds no transferable individual weighting on the real data (individual weights do worse than the population's), and in an exploratory, post-hoc calibration the real data fall below panels with injected aspect-weight heterogeneity worth +1.4 and +5 oracle points. MultiPref's per-annotation aspect labels bound one source of content heterogeneity: the aspects conflict in only 4 \% of held-out comparisons, so personalising how raters weigh their own aspect judgements gains 0.1 points of direction accuracy (in-sample 0.4) over a pooled weighting that already gets 97.5 \% right. We recommend graded labels, comparisons sampled for value conflicts, new-rater evaluation with a permutation control, separate calibration and ranking metrics, and a scale-use baseline.
\end{abstract}
\section{Introduction}

Reward models and LLM judges are trained on human ratings that disagree. A recent line of work treats this disagreement as signal to be modelled rather than noise to be averaged: distributional preference learning estimates a distribution over each alternative's utility rather than a point score (\citealp{siththaranjan2024dpl}); jury learning predicts individual annotators from content, annotator and demographic-group embeddings (\citealp{gordon2022jury}), and large datasets such as PRISM are analysed by sociodemographic group (\citealp{kirk2024prism}); personalised reward models learn a per-user latent, inferred for a new user from a few examples (\citealp{poddar2024vpl}; \citealp{chen2025pal}). The pluralistic-alignment agenda (\citealp{sorensen2024roadmap}) makes the representation of individual and group differences a first-class goal.

What these models learn about a person is rarely tested directly. Evaluations typically use synthetic users whose preferences differ by construction, binary preference labels, or users seen in training. We ask a simple question under a common protocol on real raters: \textbf{given k ratings from someone the model has never seen, how much better can it predict their other ratings --- in particular, which response they prefer --- and what is it that it learns?} The answer we find is that the methods learn calibration, not ranking --- and a semi-synthetic positive control shows how strong a rater's content preferences would have to be before they learn ranking.

Measurement theory suggests a candidate answer that reward modelling largely ignores: raters differ in \textbf{how they use the response scale} --- some say ``clearly better'' where others say ``slightly better'', some rarely call a tie, some are harsher across the board (\citealp{linacre1989mfrm}; \citealp{baumgartner2001response}). Such response styles are text-independent, cheap to estimate, and invisible to models that condition only on content or demographics. We make them a reference point for calibration. Because they are text-independent they barely change which response a rater is predicted to prefer (only through asymmetric ``clearly''/``slightly'' mass); a personalised reward model that learns content preferences should therefore show its value in ranking, and a model whose likelihood gain does not exceed the scale-use offsets has not shown that it learns more than scale use.

\textbf{Contributions.} (1) A learner-free measurement of where the predictable structure of multi-rater preference data sits --- split-half reliability of the rating distribution's shape, cross-fitted text decodability, and a rater--item decomposition of held-out likelihood (\S{}3). (2) A three-parameter scale-use baseline that plugs into any reward model with graded outputs (\S{}4). (3) A systematic new-rater test, on three datasets, of group-conditioned, low-rank-latent, published (VPL, PAL), in-context and fine-tuned personalised reward models against that baseline, documented in an analysis log of timestamped internal protocols, with post-result changes marked as amendments and failures reported (\S{}5, \S{}9). (4) Two limits of what personalisation buys --- it disappears on binary labels and it is mostly calibration rather than ranking --- and recommendations for the field (\S{}6--7). (5) A semi-synthetic positive control with a permutation contrast that measures how strong content preferences must be for these methods to detect them, and places the real data on that scale (\S{}5.6). (6) A data-grounded ceiling on ranking personalisation from annotators' own aspect labels: value conflicts are rare, so the room for content personalisation is small (\S{}5.7).

\section{Related work}

\textbf{Pluralistic and distributional reward models.} Distributional preference learning (\citealp{siththaranjan2024dpl}), diverging-preference detection (\citealp{zhang2025diverging}), PRISM (\citealp{kirk2024prism}), MultiPref (\citealp{miranda2025hybrid}) and DICES (\citealp{aroyo2023dices}) motivate modelling disagreement; jury learning (\citealp{gordon2022jury}) and multi-annotator models (\citealp{davani2022dealing}) predict individual annotators. \textbf{Personalised reward models} infer a user latent from a few examples (\citealp{poddar2024vpl}; \citealp{chen2025pal}), represent users in a low-rank reward subspace (\citealp{bose2025lore}), induce persona prompts from a user's history (\citealp{ryan2025synthesizeme}, who also release a benchmark of PRISM and Chatbot Arena users), or learn per-user embeddings jointly with the reward model (\citealp{li2024prlhf}); VPL's latent can collapse under sparse preference data (\citealp{kim2026swap}). A multi-faceted comparison of personalisation methods (\citealp{dong2025personalization}) reports large differences between methods and safety costs. Annotator embeddings (\citealp{deng2023annotate}) and per-annotator heads (\citealp{davani2022dealing}) are close relatives of our offsets; our contribution is not the offset model but the new-rater test and what it shows. Rater groups have been found to use safety severity scales differently (\citealp{mishra2026decoding}), which is consistent with the leniency component we find on DICES. \textbf{Annotator demographics} as predictors of labels have been found weak in several studies (e.g.~\citealp{orlikowski2023ecological}). \textbf{Measurement theory}: rater severity and scale use are standard facets in many-facet Rasch measurement (\citealp{linacre1989mfrm}; \citealp{eckes2011mfrm}) and response styles are a classical survey-methods topic (\citealp{baumgartner2001response}). We connect the last line to the first: for new-rater prediction, the measurement-theory rater facet is what the reward model is missing. \textbf{Collaborative filtering and annotator models.} User and item bias terms carry much of the predictive gain in collaborative filtering (\citealp{koren2009mf}), and user-specific ordinal thresholds predict personal rating distributions (\citealp{koren2011ordrec}); annotator models estimate rater-specific labelling behaviour (\citealp{dawid1979em}; \citealp{raykar2010crowds}; \citealp{hovy2013mace}), and the label-variation literature argues for modelling it (\citealp{plank2022problem}; \citealp{uma2021learning}). Our offsets are of this kind and are not new; what is new is testing personalised reward models against them on new raters, and measuring what content personalisation these models could detect at all.

\section{Data, and where the predictable structure sits}

\textbf{Data.} MultiPref (\citealp{miranda2025hybrid}; 10,461 comparisons; 4 ratings each by 2 crowd and 2 expert raters; 227 persistent raters; 5 ordered levels A-clearly \ldots{} B-clearly). PRISM (\citealp{kirk2024prism}; 68,371 responses each rated 1--100 by one of 1,396 users; binned to 5 fixed levels). DICES-350 (\citealp{aroyo2023dices}; 350 conversations fully crossed with 104 quality-checked raters; safe / unsure / unsafe).

\textbf{A learner-free measurement (Appendix D).} Before fitting any personalised model we measured, on MultiPref, where held-out predictable structure sits: beyond item location, rater-specific thresholds improve held-out likelihood about 25 times more than item-specific dispersion (+0.036 vs +0.0014 nats per rating), the item-level shape of the rating distribution that replicates across disjoint rater panels is small (split-half reliability 0.25), and about a tenth of it is decodable from text. Beyond item location, the predictable structure is therefore mostly rater-side scale use, while the item-level shape that replicates across disjoint panels is small and only partly in the text. These two facts come from different estimands and do not say that disagreement is ``not in the text''. The stage-1 decision rule we had written down returned \textbf{``mixed''}, because G/X was not $\leq$ 10 \% (interval 5.7--11.9 \%); the factorised model of \S{}4 was chosen with that outcome known.

\section{A three-parameter scale-use baseline}
\begin{figure*}[t]\centering\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{fig_styles.pdf}
\caption{What three numbers per rater change. Left: the marginal distribution of MultiPref ratings. Right: the same distribution passed through the fitted scale-use offsets (extreme, slight, tie) of three real new raters (k = 50) closest to the 90th / 10th percentile of intensity (extreme minus slight) among raters whose tie offset is at or below the median, and closest to the 90th percentile of the tie offset. The offsets are text-independent: they reshape how strongly a rater is predicted to prefer, not which side.}\label{fig:styles}\end{figure*}

(Figure 2 illustrates the effect of the offsets.) Let $\ell$(x) $\in$ R\^{}K be the class logits of a text-only distributional reward model for item x (K ordered rating levels). For rater r we add a text-independent offset vector: p(y \textbar{} x, r) = softmax($\ell$(x) + b\_r). Variants: \textbf{DF} (b\_r $\in$ R\^{}K, free), \textbf{DFs} (two-sided preference scales only: b\_r tied symmetrically, (e, s, t, s, e) --- extreme, slight, tie --- 3 parameters, of which 2 are identifiable because the softmax is invariant to a common shift; no A-vs-B position term). Rater offsets have a Gaussian prior centred on the training-rater mean; a new rater's offsets are the MAP estimate from k of their ratings with the text model frozen. Baselines under the same protocol: \textbf{D} (text-only distributional RM), \textbf{DG} (group offsets: crowd/expert, race, region), \textbf{DL} (low-rank text-interacting latent: $\ell$(x) + (Wx + w$_0$)$\cdot$z\_r, d = 8, the personalised-RM family). An interpretable probit family (rater shift / log-scale on an ordered-probit text head) is reported as a secondary analysis.

\textbf{Training and adaptation.} The text model and the training raters' offsets are fitted jointly by penalised maximum likelihood, \[\min \sum_{(i,r,y)} -\log p(y \mid x_i, r) + \lambda_{\text{text}} \|V\|^2 + \lambda_r \sum_r \|b_r\|^2,\] with \(\lambda_{\text{text}}\) chosen on the text-only model and \(\lambda_r\) on held-out items of the training raters. A new rater \(r^*\) is adapted from \(k\) ratings \(\{(x_j, y_j)\}\) by \(\hat b_{r^*} = \arg\min_b \sum_j -\log \mathrm{softmax}(\ell(x_j) + b)_{y_j} + \lambda_r \|b - \bar b\|^2\), with \(\bar b\) the training-rater mean (the prior mean; \(\hat b = \bar b\) at \(k = 0\)) and the text model frozen --- a convex problem in 3--5 parameters, solved in milliseconds. Any text model that outputs graded log-probabilities can be used: we use a ridge head on frozen LLM features (\S{}5.1--5.2), a LoRA-fine-tuned LLM reward model (\S{}5.3) and the decoder of a published personalised reward model (\S{}5.4).

\section{Experiments}
\begin{figure*}[t]\centering\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{fig_main.pdf}
\caption{New raters: gain in held-out log-likelihood per rating over the text-only distributional reward model (D), as a function of the number k of the new rater's ratings used for adaptation; 95\% rater-cluster bootstrap bands, 5 folds. Text-independent rater offsets (DF; symmetric three-parameter DFs on two-sided preferences) gain 0.08--0.10 nats per rating at k = 20 on all three datasets; group offsets (crowd/expert, race, region) gain nothing; a low-rank text-interacting rater latent with a few-shot-tuned prior ties the three-parameter DFs and beats the five-offset DF on MultiPref (DF $-$ DL = $-$0.015). \textbf{Protocol.} 5 folds; in each, 20 \% of raters (stratified by group) and 20 \% of prompt groups are held out; models are trained on training raters $\times$ training items with penalties chosen on a validation split; each test rater is adapted on k $\in$ \{0, 5, 10, 20, 50\} of their ratings of \emph{training} items (5 random draws) and evaluated on their ratings of \emph{held-out} items. Primary metric: held-out log-likelihood per rating; also RPS and preference-direction accuracy; rater-cluster bootstrap (B = 2,000). Because test raters share test items on MultiPref and DICES, the headline k = 20 contrasts were also re-estimated with a two-way bootstrap that resamples raters and prompt groups independently: on graded labels every log-likelihood interval widens by at most 0.007 on either side and no log-likelihood conclusion changes; direction-accuracy differences of about a point or less (the style model's +0.5 on MultiPref, VPL's direction advantage over the style model) lose significance and are marked where they appear. Text model in \S{}5.1--5.2: frozen Qwen2.5-7B features with a ridge head; \S{}5.3: a LoRA-fine-tuned reward model.

\subsection{On a fine-tuned reward model}

With a LoRA-fine-tuned Qwen2.5-7B reward model (D-LoRA, trained per fold on the training raters' rating distributions) as the text model, the three-number style model still adds +0.100 {[}+0.071, +0.130{]} nats per rating at k = 20 --- the same as its gain on the frozen-feature head (+0.100). Fine-tuning improves the text-only model by +0.027 {[}+0.016, +0.039{]} (direction accuracy +1.4 points) and the style model by the same +0.027: the two gains add. Personalising the frozen-feature model with 20 ratings per rater gains more than fine-tuning the text model (+0.072 {[}+0.041, +0.106{]}). DFs keeps the direction accuracy of D-LoRA (+0.002 {[}$-$0.002, +0.006{]}). Against in-context personalisation --- a separate LoRA model on the same base, trained with ten of the rater's other ratings in the prompt and evaluated with the new rater's k ratings in the prompt --- the three offsets on D-LoRA are better at k = 20. The in-context model's result varies with its training seed (two of fifteen runs failed, gaining $-$0.078 and $-$0.055 over D-LoRA against +0.021 to +0.125 for the others; the two failed runs have the highest training loss), so it was trained with three seeds per fold, and we fixed in advance that ``better'' requires a lower bound above zero for every seed and for the seed average. Averaged over seeds --- the expected result of one training run --- the offsets are better by +0.041 {[}+0.027, +0.054{]} nats per rating (209 raters; per seed +0.062, +0.018 and +0.042, every lower bound at least +0.005) and more accurate on which response the rater prefers (+1.9 points {[}+0.9, +2.7{]}). The in-context model does learn from the history (+0.051 {[}+0.024, +0.079{]} over D-LoRA on average), at several thousand prompt tokens per prediction, but its direction accuracy is below D-LoRA's ($-$1.7 points {[}$-$2.6, $-$0.8{]} on average; $-$0.6 to $-$3.0 per seed).

\subsection{Published personalised reward models}

We reimplemented VPL (\citealp{poddar2024vpl}) and PAL-B (\citealp{chen2025pal}) from their official code as heads on the same frozen per-response embeddings and evaluated them under the same new-rater protocol, refitting the text-only and style models on those embeddings. On graded labels, at k = 20 the style model is better than VPL by +0.092 {[}+0.068, +0.119{]} nats per rating ({[}+0.063, +0.124{]} when prompt groups are also resampled) and than PAL-B by +0.158. VPL's latent does carry rater information once its posterior collapse is prevented (KL weight selected as 0 or 0.001; +0.087 over its own unpersonalised version), but only lifts it to the level of the text-only model; PAL-B's few-shot user weights give no gain. Their user representations are unrelated to response style (cross-validated R$^2$ $\approx$ 0 against the style offsets). On binary labels --- the setting in which both methods were proposed --- every method's personalisation gain is at most 0.002 nats (small but nonzero for PAL-B with K = 4, and for VPL and the style model at k = 50), and VPL's text head is the best binary model (the style model is 0.010 nats behind it and 0.005 behind the text-only model, differences of the size of item-sampling noise): the individual differences that are predictable for new raters live in the graded levels and ties that binary preference data discard. VPL's per-response reward head is slightly better at predicting \emph{which} response is preferred (0.677 vs 0.668), an effect present without personalisation and not significant once prompt groups are also resampled (style model $-$ VPL: $-$0.9 points {[}$-$2.7, +0.8{]}). The offsets are also a plug-in: added to VPL (fitted on the same k ratings, VPL frozen) they improve it by +0.088 {[}+0.066, +0.111{]} nats per rating at k = 20, tie with the style model on likelihood ($-$0.004 {[}$-$0.014, +0.005{]}) and keep VPL's direction accuracy (+1.1 points over the style model alone; rater bootstrap only).

\subsection{Could these methods have detected content personalisation? A semi-synthetic positive control}

A ranking null is informative only if the methods would detect content-dependent preferences where they exist. We keep MultiPref's structure (raters, items, who rated what, folds and k-shot draws) and replace the labels by semi-synthetic ones. A generator --- the text-only head plus scale-use offsets, fitted on all ratings --- gives each rating a distribution, to which a rater-specific content term \((\theta_r^\top g_i)\,(-2,-1,0,1,2)\) is added to the logits; \(g_i\) are two features of the response pair (the log length ratio and a random direction in the per-response embedding space, orthogonal to the consensus quality direction) and \(\theta_r \sim N(0, \tau^2 I)\). The scale \(\tau\) is calibrated on the generator alone so that an oracle that knows \(\theta_r\) gains +1.7, +5.2, +10.1 or +21.1 points of direction accuracy on the test ratings; \(\tau = 0\) is a null control. Every method is then run under the paper's protocol. Because a method whose unpersonalised state is not the population state can improve with anyone's ratings, the contrast is \textbf{the method adapted on the rater's own k ratings minus the same method given another test rater's adapted parameters} (a random derangement within fold, k and draw). Before the runs we fixed that the protocol has power at a strength if some content-interacting method has a lower 95 \% bound above +1 point at k = 20; the +10 and +21 panels and the untuned-prior low-rank runs were added after the first results and are exploratory.

\subsection{How much room is there? Value conflicts are rare}

The positive control asks what the methods could detect; MultiPref also lets us ask how much there is to detect. Every MultiPref annotation carries the annotator's own helpfulness, truthfulness and harmlessness preferences next to the overall one, so a rater's way of trading these values off is observable. We fit an ordinal model of the overall preference from the annotator's own aspect labels (their values and absolute values) with rater-specific cut-points (scale use) and either a pooled trade-off or a rater-specific trade-off, adapted on k of the rater's training-item annotations and evaluated on held-out items \textbf{with the rater's own aspect labels as inputs} --- an oracle for the content side. The pooled trade-off weights harmlessness most (6.1, five-fold mean), then helpfulness (4.0) and truthfulness (2.0). With the rater's own aspect labels as inputs the pooled model already gets 97.5 \% of directions right (74 \% on conflict items): aspect and overall labels are produced together by the same annotator, so this ceiling concerns a small residual and is a property of MultiPref's concurrent labelling.

Even with the rater's own aspect judgements and all of their training ratings, a rater-specific trade-off predicts which response they prefer only 0.1 points better than the population's trade-off, and even fitted in-sample only 0.4 points better. The reason is that the aspects point in opposite directions in only 4.0 \% of the held-out non-tie comparisons (3.5 \% of all annotations); there individual trade-offs matter a great deal (in-sample +9.7 points; among the 20 raters with at least ten helpfulness--harmlessness conflicts, the share of conflicts resolved in favour of helpfulness ranges from 0.00 to 0.94), but they are too rare to move overall accuracy. Individual trade-offs do change how strongly raters prefer (up to +0.046 nats with all their ratings), in line with the calibration-not-ranking pattern. We fixed in advance that an aspect-weight personalised reward model would be built only if the lower bound of this ceiling exceeded half a point with all training ratings; it does not, so we did not build one. The learned personalisers cannot be tested on the conflict items either: the 243 held-out non-tie ratings on them give own $-$ other intervals wider than the achievable ceiling (k = 20: low-rank latent +0.1 {[}$-$2.2, +2.4{]}). \textbf{A calibrated test on the real data.} The ceiling above gives each rater's own aspect labels; a deployable personaliser would know only the item's aspects. We therefore ran the reference learner of \S{}5.6 on the real labels with the item's consensus aspect differences from its \emph{other} annotators as content features (3-d), with a hierarchical prior centred at the population weighting (estimated on the training raters) and compared each rater's own weights with that population weighting (style offsets kept). Individual weights transfer \emph{worse} than the population's: $-$0.28 {[}$-$0.56, $-$0.06{]} points at k = 20 and $-$0.63 {[}$-$1.01, $-$0.25{]} at k = 50. With injected aspect-weight heterogeneity it gives +0.46 {[}$-$0.00, +0.96{]} at k = 50 when an oracle would gain +1.4 points and +3.4 {[}+2.6, +4.3{]} at +5.0. The real data lie well below the +5-point panel and, at k = 50, just below the +1.4-point panel: transferable heterogeneity in how raters weigh these three values is at most of the order of one to two oracle points on MultiPref. This test is exploratory and post hoc, and its contrast was revised four times (Appendix A; no version shows a positive transferable aspect part), the calibration strengths were chosen after seeing the real bound, it uses one synthetic draw on panels noisier than the real data, and it uses other annotators' aspect labels of the test items (an aspect oracle, not a deployable model). Three caveats: the ceiling bounds personalisation of the \emph{weighting} only --- disagreement about which response is more helpful or harmless is absorbed into the inputs; it covers content preferences expressible through these three aspects only; MultiPref's prompt sampling determines how often values conflict; and annotators may have aligned their aspect labels with their overall label, which would understate conflicts.

\section{What personalisation buys: calibration, not ranking}

\textbf{Binary labels remove the signal.} On MultiPref with ties dropped and labels binarised, every method's personalisation gain is at most 0.002 nats per rating (\S{}5.4), and the scale-use baseline's binary accuracy equals the text-only model's ($-$0.0002). The individual differences that are predictable for new raters live in the graded levels and in ties --- exactly what binary preference data discard, and the format in which most personalised reward models are trained and evaluated. \textbf{Ranking moves little.} Text-independent offsets change how strongly a rater is predicted to prefer a response, not which response: on MultiPref the scale-use baseline raises direction accuracy by only +0.005 {[}+0.001, +0.008{]} at k = 20 ({[}$-$0.001, +0.011{]} with items also resampled; about half of it, +0.002 {[}+0.000, +0.005{]}, is already present at k = 0 and comes from training the text head jointly with the offsets) (and the 5-offset version with an A-vs-B term lowers it by 0.014), while RPS drops from 0.1593 to 0.1532. On single-response safety ratings the same offsets do move the safe/unsafe decision (+4.8 points on DICES), because there a rater's threshold \emph{is} the decision. Content-interacting personalisation does not do better on ranking: VPL's advantage in direction accuracy is present without personalisation (k = 0), comes from its text head, and is within item-sampling noise. How strong a content preference would have to be for these methods to detect it is measured in \S{}5.6. Against its own k = 0 version, no method's direction accuracy on MultiPref rises by more than 0.2 points at k = 20, and every upper 95 \% bound stays below +0.8 points even when prompt groups are resampled together with raters (VPL +0.07 {[}$-$0.57, +0.74{]}, style model $-$0.10 {[}$-$0.69, +0.54{]}, PAL-B $-$0.48 {[}$-$1.11, +0.14{]}). \textbf{The ranking null replicates on real users' own choices.} PRISM users each saw 2--4 responses to their own prompt, scored them and chose one; pairing the chosen response with every other one gives 41,199 native pairwise choices of 1,396 users. Under the same new-user protocol (5 user folds $\times$ 5 conversation folds, k of the user's pairs from training conversations), we fixed before any result that the ranking null would be stated only if every method's improvement in choice accuracy over its own k = 0 version had an upper 95 \% bound below one point at k = 20. It holds for all of them (1,033 new users): VPL +0.12 points {[}$-$0.08, +0.33{]}, PAL-B +0.0 {[}$-$0.4, +0.4{]} (K = 2) and +0.3 {[}$-$0.1, +0.7{]} (K = 4), the 5-offset model $-$0.6 {[}$-$1.4, +0.2{]}; the symmetric scale-use model is identical to the text-only model on binary choices, because a binary label carries no intensity. LoRe (\citealp{bose2025lore}), which reports +10 points for unseen PRISM users, run here with its own few-shot fit and with a validated adaptation ridge under the same pre-set rule, also fails it: own gain at k = 20 $-$1.5 {[}$-$2.5, $-$0.4{]} (regularised $-$0.5 {[}$-$1.2, +0.2{]}), own $-$ other +0.2 {[}$-$0.8, +1.2{]}. The published setting differs in embeddings, baseline, pair construction and split. With a graded label cut from the users' own score differences (thresholds fixed in advance), the MultiPref pattern returns at half the size: at k = 20 the scale-use model gains +0.043 {[}+0.031, +0.057{]} nats per rating over its k = 0 version, VPL +0.015 {[}+0.005, +0.027{]}, PAL-B 0.000; the scale-use model is ahead of VPL by +0.045 {[}+0.030, +0.062{]}; VPL plus the offsets gains +0.060 and ties the scale-use model ($-$1.4360 vs $-$1.4354); and no method's own direction-accuracy gain exceeds +0.2 points (the 5-offset model loses 1.7).

\section{Recommendations}

\begin{enumerate}
\def\labelenumi{(\roman{enumi})}
\tightlist
\item
  Collect and keep graded labels (and ties) when individual differences matter; binary preferences erase most of what is predictable about a new rater. Sample comparisons for value conflicts (for example, pairs on which aspect ratings disagree): in MultiPref only 4 \% of comparisons involve one, and they are where individual trade-offs matter. (ii) Report a scale-use calibration baseline (a few text-independent offsets per rater) next to any personalised reward model. Beating it on likelihood is necessary but not sufficient evidence of content personalisation; a method that learns content preferences should show it in ranking or choice outcomes, where the offsets cannot help by construction. (iii) Evaluate on raters unseen in training, adapted from k of their own ratings, and on held-out items, and compare each method with itself given another rater's ratings (a permutation control): a poor unpersonalised state inflates gains measured against k = 0. (iv) Report calibration (log-likelihood, RPS) and ranking (direction accuracy) separately: they answer different questions --- how strongly someone prefers, and what to show them. (v) Check what a learned user representation encodes; in the two published models we reimplemented it was unrelated to scale use (R$^2$ $\approx$ 0) and carried little else that transferred to new raters.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Discussion}

\textbf{Why did published personalised models learn little about new raters here?} Their evaluations differ from ours in three ways that each favour content-dependent personalisation: synthetic users whose preferences differ in content by construction, binary labels that remove scale use, and users seen during training. On real raters, graded labels and unseen users, the structure that transfers from k ratings to new items is mostly how the rater uses the scale, which neither a content-interacting latent (VPL) nor a mixture of content prototypes (PAL-B) represents (R$^2$ $\approx$ 0 against the offsets). Adding the offsets to VPL recovers the whole gain (+0.088): the two are complementary, and a content-personalising method should be shown to beat VPL plus offsets. The positive control (\S{}5.6) adds a second reason: these methods miss content preferences that a learner told the right features recovers from the same 20 ratings.

\textbf{What this does and does not say about pluralism (moved from \S{}8).} Our results do not show that people agree about content: the item-level disagreement that replicates across rater panels is real (split-half reliability 0.25 on MultiPref) and about a tenth of it is decodable from text. They show that, for predicting an individual new rater from few ratings, that content-level disagreement is a small part of what is predictable, and that demographic or crowd/expert groups add nothing beyond the text.

\section{Where is the predictable structure? Estimators and results}

\textbf{Estimators.} For an item with ratings on \(K\) ordered levels, let \(h\) be the empirical rating distribution and \(C\) the cumulative operator. Split the item's raters at random into disjoint panels \(P_1\) (\(a\) raters) and \(P_2\), and let \(q\) be a cross-fitted \emph{location-only} forecast with exactly the mode and mean of \(P_1\) (built from other items in the same mode-and-mean cell). With \(r_1 = C(h_1 - q)\), \(r_2 = C(h_2 - q)\), \(M = \|r_1\|^2/n\) and \(X = \langle r_1, r_2\rangle/n\), the forecasting value of the raw distribution over its own location obeys the identity \(S(h_1) - S(q) = M - 2X\) under the ranked probability score, so \(\rho = X/M\) is a split-half reliability of the distribution's \emph{shape} and the raw distribution beats its location exactly when \(\rho > 1/2\). Text decodability is the proper-score gain \(G = (2\langle \hat r, r_2\rangle - \|\hat r\|^2)/n\) of a residual \(\hat r\) decoded from frozen text features, cross-fitted over prompt groups, reported as the fraction \(G/X\) of the reproducible shape. The rater--item decomposition fits ordered-probit models \(Y^*_{ir} = \theta_i + \delta_r + \sigma_i\sigma_r\varepsilon\) with item locations \(\theta_i\), rater shifts \(\delta_r\), rater scales \(\sigma_r\) and item dispersions \(\sigma_i\), compares held-out likelihood over random cells (every held-out rater and item appears in training), and chooses all penalties by inner cross-validation.

\textbf{Reproducibility.} With the split-half estimator of a companion study (anonymised), the reliability of the rating distribution's shape beyond its exact mode and mean is $\rho$ = 0.25 {[}0.22, 0.29{]} on MultiPref overall preference with three raters (helpful 0.29, harmless 0.21): the raw few-rater distribution forecasts a new rater worse than its own location ($\rho$ \textless{} $\tfrac12$). \textbf{Text decodability.} A cross-fitted ridge decoder on frozen Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct features, scored on an independent rater panel, recovers G/X = 10.5 \% {[}5.7 \%, 11.9 \%{]} of the reproducible shape (within-cell permutation control $\leq$ 0; positive control 0.35) --- comparable to the upper end for ordinal quality ratings (upper bounds 0.4--10 \%) and below NLI (17--26 \%). \textbf{Rater vs item.} A long-format ordered-probit decomposition with real rater identities and penalties tuned by inner cross-validation: rater-specific thresholds improve held-out likelihood by +0.036 nats per rating over item location alone, item-specific dispersion by a further +0.0014 (ratio 25; 19--36 across the five folds); of the rater part, rater scale contributes +0.031 and rater shift +0.005.

All analyses are documented in an analysis log: timestamped internal protocols written before each stage (not public registrations, so they document rather than certify the order of decisions); changes after results are logged as amendments with reasons, and the symmetric style model DFs and its hypothesis were added after the first full result.
